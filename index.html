The same applies when we study the events that followed the death of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH), such as the issue of the Caliphate and the conflicts that arose among the Companions; understanding the tribal structure helps us to analyze these events more deeply and objectively.

Key Concept: The Pre-Islamic World

This session explores the global conditions—religious, moral, social, and economic—especially in Arabia, before Prophet Muhammad's (PBUH) mission, highlighting the need for divine guidance.

Quranic and Prophetic Depiction of the World Before Islam

The Quran and the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) have depicted the state of the world before Islam in eloquent and impactful ways. Allah the Almighty says in His decisive Book, describing the state of the Arabs in particular:

"And remember the favor of Allah upon you - when you were enemies and He brought your hearts together and you became, by His favor, brothers. And you were on the edge of a pit of the Fire, and He saved you from it. Thus does Allah make clear to you His verses that you may be guided." (Quran, Al 'Imran آل عمران: 103)

This noble verse depicts how they were on the brink of an abyss, about to fall into the fire of misery and destruction, had not the mercy of Allah reached them with the mission of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH).

As for the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH), he summarized this bitter reality for us in his noble Hadith, narrated by Imam Muslim from 'Iyad ibn Himar al-Mujashi'i (عيَاض بن حِمَار المُجَاشِعِي - May Allah be pleased with him), that the Messenger of Allah (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) said one day in his sermon:

"Behold, my Lord commanded me to teach you what you were ignorant of, from what He taught me this day of mine... (until he said) And indeed, Allah looked at the people of the earth and despised them (مَقَتَهُمْ - maqatahum), their Arabs and their non-Arabs, except for remnants of the People of the Book..."

"Al-Maqt" (المقت) means the most severe hatred and wrath. This Hadith indicates that the anger of Allah Almighty had encompassed most of the people of the earth, their Arabs and their non-Arabs, due to their state of polytheism (الشرك - ash-Shirk), corruption, and deviation. Only a very few of the People of the Book were saved from this wrath, those who still adhered to remnants of the monotheistic religion (دين التوحيد - din at-Tawhid) brought by their prophets, even though their religion had also been subjected to much distortion and alteration.

Humanity, O brothers, was living in one of the darkest periods of its history. Noble human values had vanished or were on the verge of vanishing, and injustice, ignorance, and corruption had spread everywhere. There was no voice of truth to be heard, nor a beacon of guidance to be followed. The previous heavenly religions had been subjected to distortion and corruption, and had lost much of their original essence. Christianity, which Jesus (عيسى - 'Isa, peace be upon him) brought as a message of love, peace, and monotheism, had deviated from its course, and doctrines of the Trinity, worship of saints and images, had entered it, and it had moved away from the simplicity of monotheism to which Christ had called. Judaism, which Allah revealed to Moses (موسى - Musa, peace be upon him) as a law and guidance, had turned into a closed, racial religion, and its scholars and rabbis had distorted the words of Allah, and sold them for a paltry price. As for the rest of the nations, they had drowned in the mires of paganism, worshipping idols, planets, animals, and even humans.

Humanity yearned for a savior and looked forward to a light that would dispel this oppressive darkness. The human فطرة (fitrah - innate disposition), which Allah created upon monotheism, was suffering under the weight of this polytheism and misguidance. Thus, the mission of Prophet Muhammad (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) was a response to this urgent need, and a mercy from Allah to the worlds.

The Conditions of the Arabs Before Islam: Detail and Expansion

Now, let us delve into the details of the conditions of the Arabs before Islam, to paint a clearer picture of the society that witnessed the dawn of prophethood.

First: The Economic Aspect and Prevailing Livelihoods (Detail and Analysis)

Economic life in the Arabian Peninsula, that vast land whose topography varies between arid deserts, towering mountains, and green oases, was characterized by diversity and contrast, but overall, it was a simple life, dependent on limited natural resources, and greatly affected by harsh climatic conditions.

Desert Dwellers and the Life of Pastoralism and Nomadism (The Dominant Lifestyle)

Bedouin pastoralists constituted the vast majority of the population of the Arabian Peninsula. Their lives revolved around herds of camels, sheep, and goats, which were their primary source of livelihood. Theirs was not a life of stability, but one of constant wandering, moving from place to place in search of "الربع" (ar-Raba' - areas of pasture and water) for their livestock. This lifestyle imposed upon them a certain harshness and toughness of character, and the ability to endure hardships and difficulties. They lived in simple tents made of goat hair or camel-hair, which were easy to transport, dismantle, and set up. Their food depended heavily on the milk and meat of their livestock, in addition to dates obtained from oases, and some wild plants that grew in the desert. They did not have organized agriculture, except in some scattered oases, nor did they have advanced industries; their industries were simple handicrafts to meet their daily needs, such as making tents, leather goods, and some simple weapons.

Trade among them was limited, confined to the exchange of some basic goods. This lifestyle, although it endowed them with some positive qualities such as courage, generosity, and patience, also made them live in a state of relative poverty and lack of many of the components of civilization and material progress. Their lives were subject to the vagaries of nature, such as drought and famine, and raids by other tribes.

Urban Centers and Trading Hubs: Mecca (مكة المكرمة) as an Economic and Religious Center (Case Study)

In contrast to the Bedouin life of nomadism, some cities and urban centers in the Arabian Peninsula witnessed a degree of stability and economic prosperity. The most prominent of these cities, undoubtedly, was Mecca (مكة المكرمة - Makkah al-Mukarramah). Mecca gained its economic importance from several factors:

  • Strategic Location: Mecca was located at the crossroads of ancient trade routes, connecting the south of the Arabian Peninsula (Yemen - اليمن and Abyssinia - الحبشة) with its north (the Levant - بلاد الشام, Persia - فارس, and Egypt - مصر). This location made it an important station for trade caravans and a transit point for various goods and commodities.
  • Religious Status (The Biggest Attraction): Mecca housed the Kaaba (الكعبة المشرفة - al-Ka'bah al-Musharrafah), the Sacred House of Allah, built by Abraham (إبراهيم - Ibrahim) and Ishmael (إسماعيل - Isma'il, peace be upon them). Despite the spread of paganism and idol worship, the Arabs still venerated the Kaaba and performed Hajj (pilgrimage) to it from various parts of the Peninsula. This annual religious influx represented an important commercial season for the people of Mecca, where they would set up markets, and buy and sell with the pilgrims and those performing 'Umrah (عمرة - lesser pilgrimage).
  • Organization of Trade (The Role of Banu Hashim - بنو هاشم): Some prominent figures from Quraysh (قريش) played an important role in organizing and expanding trade. Among the most prominent of these was Hashim ibn Abd Manaf (هاشم بن عبد مناف), the grandfather of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH). Hashim was a man of insight and wisdom. He was the one who established the "الإيلاف" (al-Ilaf) for Quraysh, which involved concluding commercial treaties and pacts with tribes and kings of neighboring regions (like the Levant, Yemen, Abyssinia, and Persia) to secure the trade routes for Quraysh's caravans. He also organized the two famous trade journeys: the winter journey (رحلة الشتاء - rihlat ash-shita') which headed south to Yemen and Abyssinia, laden with goods brought from the Levant and Persia, and the summer journey (رحلة الصيف - rihlat as-saif) which headed north to the Levant, laden with products from Yemen and India. The Quran refers to these two journeys in Surah Quraysh (سورة قريش), reminding Quraysh of this great blessing:
    "For the accustomed security of Quraysh – Their accustomed security [in] the caravan of winter and summer – Let them worship the Lord of this House, Who has fed them, [saving them] from hunger and made them safe, [saving them] from fear." (Quran, Quraysh قريش: 1-4)

These factors combined made Mecca a prosperous economic center and gave Quraysh (قريش) a prestigious position among the Arab tribes. They enjoyed security and stability in their sanctuary, while the rest of the regions lived in a state of chaos and turmoil. The Quran pointed to this blessing, saying:

"Have they not seen that We made [Makkah] a safe sanctuary, while people are being snatched away all around them? Then do they believe in falsehood and deny the favor of Allah?" (Quran, Al-'Ankabut العنكبوت: 67)
Yemen (اليمن) and its Vanished Agricultural Civilization (Reasons for Decline)

Yemen (اليمن), located in the south of the Arabian Peninsula, had a rich civilizational history and was known in ancient times as "Arabia Felix" (العربية السعيدة) for its fertile land, abundant water, and flourishing agriculture. The great Ma'rib Dam (سد مأرب - Sadd Ma'rib), considered one of the wonders of ancient engineering, played a vital role in irrigating vast areas of agricultural land, contributing to the establishment of an advanced and prosperous agricultural civilization. However, this prosperity did not last. The Ma'rib Dam (سد مأرب) suffered cracks and gradual collapse due to neglect, internal conflicts, and possibly other natural factors. This deterioration culminated in the "Flood of the 'Arim" (سيل العرم - Sayl al-'Arim), a massive flood that destroyed the dam, inundated agricultural lands, and led to significant economic and social decline in Yemen. The Quran narrates this incident in Surah Saba' (سورة سبأ) as a lesson and admonition for those who would take heed:

"There was for [the tribe of] Saba' in their dwelling place a sign: two [fields of] gardens on the right and on the left. [They were told], 'Eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful to Him. A good land [have you], and a forgiving Lord.' But they turned away [refusing], so We sent upon them the flood of the dam, and We replaced their two [fields of] gardens with gardens of bitter fruit, tamarisks and something of sparse lote trees. That We recompensed them for what they denied, and do We recompense except the ungrateful?" (Quran, Saba' سبأ: 15-17)

This catastrophic event led to the dispersal of the people of Yemen to various parts of the Arabian Peninsula and beyond, and the migration of many Yemeni tribes northward, weakening Yemen's economic and political strength.

Yathrib (يثرب - Medina المنورة) as an Agricultural Oasis (The Role of the Ansar - الأنصار)

Yathrib (يثرب), which later became the first capital of the Islamic state and Medina (المدينة المنورة - al-Madinah al-Munawwarah), was located in a fertile area north of Mecca. It was famous for its abundant water and fertile soil, making it an important agricultural oasis. Its people, especially the tribes of Aws (الأوس) and Khazraj (الخزرج) (later known as the Ansar - الأنصار), relied heavily on agriculture, particularly date palm cultivation, which was an important source of food and income. They also grew some grains and vegetables. The Ansar (الأنصار) were known for their skill in agriculture and were people of hard work and diligence.

Summary of the General Economic Situation

In general, it can be said that economic life in the Arabian Peninsula before Islam was characterized by stark contrasts. While some areas, such as Mecca (مكة), Yathrib (يثرب), and Yemen (اليمن) (in its prosperous eras), enjoyed a degree of stability and relative prosperity, the vast majority of the population, especially in the deserts, lived a harsh and difficult life, dependent on limited resources, and greatly affected by the fluctuations of nature. There was no unified currency; trade was conducted through barter or by using foreign currencies like the Persian Dirham (الدرهم الفارسي) and the Roman Dinar (الدينار الروماني). There were no developed financial or economic institutions. This fragile economic situation had its repercussions on social and moral life, as we shall see.

Recap: Economic Landscape

  • Bedouins: Pastoral nomadism, harsh life, limited trade.
  • Mecca: Trade hub (Ilaf, winter/summer caravans), religious center (Kaaba).
  • Yemen: Past agricultural glory (Ma'rib Dam), decline due to dam collapse.
  • Yathrib (Medina): Agricultural oasis (dates, grains), home to Aws & Khazraj.
  • Overall: Contrasting conditions, resource scarcity, lack of unified economy.

Second: The Social and Moral Aspect: A Mosaic of Contradictions (Detailed and In-depth Analysis)

The social and moral fabric in the pre-Islamic Arabian Peninsula was a complex mosaic, where the dark colors of vices and deviations intertwined with some bright glimmers of noble values and ethical virtues. Social life did not follow a single pattern but was influenced by many factors, such as the geographical environment, tribal structure, religious beliefs, and inherited customs and traditions.

The Most Prominent Social and Moral Vices that Were Rampant (Critical Analysis)

The Jahiliyyah (الجاهلية - pre-Islamic) society suffered from many social and moral diseases that led to its deterioration and decline. Among the most prominent of these vices were:

Spread of Prohibitions and the Trade of Vice (Reflection of a Spiritual Crisis)

Prohibitions were not just isolated individual practices but were often part of the prevailing culture. Wine (الخمر - al-Khamr) was drunk in their gatherings, they boasted about it, and held festivals for it. Much mention of wine and its gatherings is found in their poetry. The wine trade was thriving, and there were no restrictions to prevent it. Allah Almighty said, describing Islam's view of wine and gambling:

"O you who have believed, indeed, intoxicants, gambling, [sacrificing on] stone alters [to other than Allah], and divining arrows are but defilement from the work of Satan, so avoid it that you may be successful." (Quran, Al-Ma'idah المائدة: 90)

Gambling (الميسر - al-Maysir) was also a common practice. They would gamble on everything, sometimes even on themselves and their families. They saw it as a kind of generosity and munificence, as the winner in gambling would distribute his winnings to the poor and needy. However, Islam forbade gambling because it involved unjustly consuming people's wealth and led to enmity and hatred. Prostitution existed in various forms and was not condemned in all circles to the same degree. This reflects the extent of moral degradation the society had reached.

The Issue of Rebuilding the Kaaba and Illicit Money (Deep Implications)

The story of rebuilding the Kaaba (الكعبة), which we mentioned earlier, carries deep implications about the moral consciousness of Quraysh (قريش). Despite their veneration for the Kaaba, they were aware that much of their wealth was acquired through illicit means. This indicates a kind of duality in their moral standards. They venerated sacred things but, at the same time, did not hesitate to earn unlawful money. When they stipulated that only "طيب" (tayyib - pure/good) money should be used in building the Kaaba, this condition in itself reflects an implicit admission of the existence of "خبيث" (khabeeth - impure/evil) money in their possessions. When the pure money was insufficient to complete the building on the foundations of Abraham (إبراهيم), this reveals the extent of illicit money circulating among them. This incident, while indicating remnants of religious scruples, also exposes the pervasiveness of financial corruption in their lives.

Adultery (الزنا - az-Zina) and its Various Forms (Collapse of Family Values)

Adultery, in its various forms, represented a serious threat to family and social values. Books of Seerah (السيرة) and historical accounts mention several types of Nikah (النكاح - marriage) that were prevalent in Jahiliyyah (الجاهلية), which can only be described as forms of institutionalized or concealed adultery.

  • Taking Akhdan (الأخدان - Secret and Forbidden Relationships): This type of relationship was common, where a man would take a woman as his secret lover (خليلة - khaleelah), or a woman would take a man as her secret lover, without a legitimate marriage contract. These relationships were often conducted in secret, but were sometimes known. Islam forbade this type of relationship and considered it explicit adultery.
  • Nikah al-Istibda' (نكاح الاستبضاع - Seeking Offspring from Someone Other Than the Husband to Improve Lineage): This was one of the strangest and most heinous types of pre-Islamic "marriage." If a man wanted to have a child with certain qualities (like courage, generosity, or eloquence) that he did not find in himself, he would ask his wife to go to another man known for these qualities and ask him to have intercourse with her. If she conceived from him, her husband would stay away from her until she delivered, and then the child would be attributed to him. This type of "marriage" reflects a demeaning view of women, considering them mere vessels for procreation, and also reflects ignorance of the laws of heredity and a disregard for honor and chastity.
  • Public Prostitution (Nikah ar-Rayat - نكاح الرايات, and Brothels): Some women, especially slave girls or those without tribal protection, practiced prostitution openly. They would plant red flags (رايات - rayat) at their doors as a sign by which their houses were known. Men would come to them from all directions and satisfy their desires for a known fee. This indicates the extent of moral degradation the society had reached, where prostitution became a declared profession in some places.
  • Nikah ar-Raht (نكاح الرهط - A Group Sharing One Woman): This was another type of pre-Islamic "marriage" where a group (رهط - raht) of men, not exceeding ten, would all have intercourse with one woman. If she conceived and gave birth, she would send for all of them, and none could refuse. When they gathered, she would say: "You know what happened, and I have given birth. He is your son, O so-and-so," naming whomever she wished among them. Her child would then be attributed to him, and the man could not refuse.
  • Nikah al-Badal (نكاح البدل - Wife Swapping): This is when a man would say to another man: "Give up your wife to me, and I will give up my wife to you," and they would exchange wives. This is one of the ugliest forms of violating honor and confusing lineage.

These types of "marriage," and others, indicate the extent of sexual chaos that prevailed in pre-Islamic society, and the absence of moral and legal controls governing relations between men and women.

Female Infanticide (وأد البنات - Wa'd al-Banat - The Crime of Killing Innocence Due to Shame or Poverty)

The custom of female infanticide (وأد البنات) is considered one of the most heinous crimes known to humanity. This custom was widespread in some Arab tribes before Islam. If a man was given the news of the birth of a daughter, his face would darken, and his heart would fill with anxiety and grief. He saw the birth of a daughter as a shame and a deficiency. Some would bury their daughters alive, either for fear of the shame she might bring upon her family if she grew up and was captured or taken prisoner, or for fear of poverty and inability to provide for her. The Quran depicted this miserable psychological state experienced by some pre-Islamic fathers:

"And when one of them is informed of [the birth of] a female, his face becomes dark, and he suppresses grief. He hides himself from the people because of the ill of which he has been informed. Should he keep it in humiliation or bury it in the dust? Unquestionably, evil is what they decide." (Quran, An-Nahl النحل: 58-59)

Islam came to eradicate this barbaric custom, to honor women, and to elevate their status. Allah Almighty threatened those who commit this crime with severe punishment, saying:

"And when the girl [who was] buried alive is asked, For what sin was she killed?" (Quran, At-Takwir التكوير: 8-9)
Killing Children for Fear of Poverty (الفقر والخوف من المستقبل - Poverty and Fear of the Future)

The crime of killing children was not limited to girls. Some Arabs would kill their children, whether male or female, for fear of poverty and inability to provide them with a livelihood. This reflects the extent of despair and despondency that controlled some souls, and lack of trust in Allah's provision and power. Islam forbade this crime and affirmed that Allah Almighty is the Provider, the Possessor of Power, the Strong, and that He has undertaken to provide for all creatures. Allah Almighty said:

"And do not kill your children out of poverty; We will provide for you and them." (Quran, Al-An'am الأنعام: 151)

And He also said:

"And do not kill your children for fear of poverty. We provide for them and for you. Indeed, their killing is ever a great sin." (Quran, Al-Isra' الإسراء: 31)
Contempt for Women and Denial of Their Basic Rights (A Demeaning View)

Women in pre-Islamic society suffered from a demeaning view and did not enjoy many of the rights that Islam granted them. They were considered inferior to men, and their opinions were often unheard.

  • Deprivation of Inheritance: Women were mostly deprived of inheritance, which was usually restricted to men capable of bearing arms and defending the tribe.
  • Marriage and Divorce Without Controls: A man could marry as many women as he wished without a maximum limit, and divorce them whenever he wished without a valid reason, and without any rights accruing to the divorced woman.
  • Dhihar (الظهار) and Ila' (الإيلاء): There were unjust customs concerning women, such as Dhihar (الظهار - when a man says to his wife, "You are to me like my mother's back," making her forbidden to him without being divorced), and Ila' (الإيلاء - when a man swears not to approach his wife for a certain period, leaving her in limbo, neither a wife nor divorced).
  • Inheriting Women Like Property: It was a custom among some Arabs that if a man died, his eldest son would inherit his father's wife (other than his own mother) just as he inherited the rest of his property. He could marry her if he wished, marry her off to whomever he wished and take her dowry, or prevent her from marrying (عضلها - 'adalaha) until she died so he could inherit her. Islam forbade this heinous custom, saying:
    "O you who have believed, it is not lawful for you to inherit women by compulsion." (Quran, An-Nisa' النساء: 19)

Islam came to honor women, restore their usurped rights, and make them sisters to men in humanity and responsibility.

Tabarruj (التبرج - Ostentatious Display) and Showing Off Adornments (Absence of Modesty and Chastity)

Tabarruj (التبرج), which is a woman displaying her adornments and charms before non-mahram (أجانب - unrelated) men, was a common phenomenon in pre-Islamic society. The Quran described this as "تَبَرُّجَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ الْأُولَىٰ" (tabarruj al-jahiliyyati al-oola - the display of the former times of ignorance) (Quran, Al-Ahzab الأحزاب: 33). Tabarruj was not limited to uncovering hair or face but included showing the chest, neck, arms, and legs, and wearing tight or transparent clothes that described the body's charms. Women would also perfume themselves, walk ostentatiously, and strike their feet to make known what they concealed of their adornment. Islam came to command women to wear Hijab (الحجاب - veil), to be modest and chaste, urged them to adhere to shyness (الحياء - al-Haya'), and forbade them from Tabarruj and displaying their adornments except to whom Allah permitted. Allah Almighty said:

"O Prophet, tell your wives and your daughters and the women of the believers to bring down over themselves [part] of their outer garments. That is more suitable that they will be known and not be abused. And ever is Allah Forgiving and Merciful." (Quran, Al-Ahzab الأحزاب: 59)

And He also said:

"...and to wrap [a portion of] their headcovers over their chests and not expose their adornment except to their husbands, their fathers, their husbands' fathers, their sons, their husbands' sons, their brothers, their brothers' sons, their sisters' sons, their women, that which their right hands possess, or those male attendants having no physical desire, or children who are not yet aware of the private aspects of women. And let them not stamp their feet to make known what they conceal of their adornment. And turn to Allah in repentance, all of you, O believers, that you might succeed." (Quran, An-Nur النور: 31)
Detestable Tribalism (العصبية القبلية المقيتة - al-'Asabiyyah al-Qabaliyyah al-Maqeetah) (Source of Conflicts and Wars)

Tribalism was the prevailing law in pre-Islamic society. An individual belonged to his tribe with absolute loyalty, was fanatically devoted to it, and supported it whether right or wrong. The tribe was the unit of social and political solidarity, and the individual derived his strength and protection from his tribe. This tribalism was a source of many conflicts and wars between tribes, where the slightest reasons were enough to ignite a war that could last for many years, shedding blood and taking lives. Islam came to eradicate this detestable tribalism and to make the bond of faith the supreme bond that unites people. Allah Almighty said:

"The believers are but brothers." (Quran, Al-Hujurat الحجرات: 10)

And the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) said:

"He is not one of us who calls to tribalism, he is not one of us who fights for tribalism, and he is not one of us who dies for tribalism." (Narrated by Abu Dawud, classed as Hasan by Al-Albani)
Plundering, Looting, Raids, and Continuous Wars (Lack of Security and Stability)

Wars and raids between Arab tribes were an integral part of their daily lives. Strong tribes would raid weaker tribes, plunder their wealth, and capture their women and children. There was no law or central authority to deter aggressors or protect the weak. These wars often arose for trivial reasons, such as disputes over a water well, a pasture, or an old vendetta. They would last for many years, leaving behind devastation and destruction. Among the most famous of these wars was the War of Basus (حرب البسوس) between the tribes of Bakr (بكر) and Taghlib (تغلب), which lasted for forty years over a she-camel. The War of Dahis and Ghabra (حرب داحس والغبراء) between the tribes of 'Abs (عبس) and Dhubyan (ذبيان) also lasted for decades. The War of Bu'ath (حرب بعاث) between the tribes of Aws (الأوس) and Khazraj (الخزرج) in Yathrib (يثرب) weakened them and made them more receptive to the call of Islam when it came. This situation of insecurity and instability had negative effects on economic and social life, making people live in a state of constant fear and anxiety.

Ethical Virtues and Noble Values (A Glimmer of Light in the Overwhelming Darkness)

Despite all these vices and deviations prevalent in pre-Islamic society, it was not entirely devoid of some noble values and ethical virtues. These values were like a glimmer of light in this overwhelming darkness, indicating that the sound human فطرة (fitrah) had not completely died, and that remnants of the religion of Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him) were still alive in some souls. Islam came to affirm and praise these virtues, to perfect and refine them, and to make them part of its integrated ethical system. Among the most prominent of these virtues were:

  • Truthfulness in Speech and Fulfilling Promises (Authentic Values): The Arabs, despite their ignorance and misguidance, prided themselves on truthfulness in speech and considered lying a defect and a disgrace. They fulfilled their promises and adhered to their covenants, even if it cost them dearly. This noble trait was manifested in many situations, even with their enemies. When Heraclius (هرقل - Hiraql), the Byzantine emperor, asked Abu Sufyan ibn Harb (أبو سفيان بن حرب - May Allah be pleased with him) (who was still a polytheist at the time) about the characteristics of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH), Abu Sufyan could not lie to him but described the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) truthfully and honestly, even though he was one of his staunchest enemies. Abu Sufyan later said: "By Allah, had it not been for the shame of being accused of lying, I would have lied about him." This shows that the value of truthfulness was deeply rooted in their souls. Similarly, fulfilling covenants was one of their most prominent traits. If an Arab gave a pledge or security, he would adhere to it, even at the cost of his own life. They despised those who broke their covenants or betrayed their trust.
  • Generosity, Munificence, and Honoring Guests (Manifestations of Arab Nobility): Arabs were famous for generosity (الكرم - al-Karam) and munificence (الجود - al-Jood). They competed in honoring and feeding guests, considering it one of the noble morals and a cause for pride and praise. They would spend all they owned for this purpose, even if it led them to poverty and need. Many examples of this overflowing generosity are found in their poetry and stories. One of the most famous generous men in Jahiliyyah (الجاهلية) was Hatim al-Ta'i (حاتم الطائي), whose generosity became proverbial.
  • Respecting and Protecting Neighbors (A Sacred Right): The neighbor (الجار - al-Jar) held a sacred status among the Arabs. They protected their neighbors, defended them, and did not tolerate injustice or harm to them. They considered aggression against a neighbor one of the greatest crimes and ugliest flaws. The Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) entered Mecca after returning from his journey to Ta'if (الطائف), where he had suffered severe harm from its people, under the protection (جوار - jiwar) of Al-Mut'im ibn 'Adi (المطعم بن عدي), who was a polytheist. Al-Mut'im protected the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH), ordered his sons to arm themselves and stand at the corners of the Kaaba, and announced that he had granted protection to Muhammad (محمد). Thus, none of Quraysh (قريش) could harm him. This shows the extent of their respect for the right of neighborliness.
  • Resilience, Patience, Courage, and Offering Aid (Qualities of the Arab Knight): The harsh desert life, with its challenges and dangers, endowed them with qualities of resilience (الجلد - al-Jalad), patience (الصبر - as-Sabr), courage (الشجاعة - ash-Shaja'ah), and اقدام (iqdam - valor). They faced difficulties bravely, endured hardships patiently, and did not fear death in defending their tribe or honor. They would aid the oppressed (النجدة - an-Najdah) and stand by them, even if they were not from their tribe. This is evident in the Hilf al-Fudul (حلف الفضول - Alliance of the Virtuous), in which some Quraysh (قريش) tribes pledged to be united with the oppressed against the oppressor until his right was restored. The Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) witnessed it before his mission and praised it after, saying: "I witnessed in the house of 'Abdullah ibn Jud'an (عبد الله بن جدعان) an alliance which I would not exchange for red camels, and if I were called to it in Islam, I would respond." This indicates that this noble value existed among them, and Islam came to affirm and strengthen it.
  • Respect and Veneration for the Sacred House (البيت الحرام) (Remnants of Hanifiyyah - الحنيفية): Despite the spread of polytheism and idol worship, the Arabs still venerated the Sacred House (البيت الحرام - al-Bayt al-Haram) and respected it immensely. They considered it the House of Allah, performed Hajj (الحج) to it, circumambulated it (الطواف - at-Tawaf), and performed some rituals inherited from their father Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him). They forbade fighting in the Sacred Months (الأشهر الحرم - al-Ash-hur al-Hurum) (Dhu al-Qa'dah ذو القعدة, Dhu al-Hijjah ذو الحجة, Muharram محرم, and Rajab رجب), considering it part of their religious observances. They would not raise arms in the Haram (الحرم - Sacred Precinct), nor kill game in it, nor cut trees in it. Even if a man met his father's killer in the Haram, he would not harm him, out of respect for the sanctity of the place.
Remnants of Abrahamic Religious Rituals (Traces of a Glorious Past)

The Arabs preserved some religious rituals inherited from their father Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him), which represented remnants of the tolerant Hanifiyyah (الحنيفية). Among these rituals were:

  • Prohibition of marrying certain close relatives, such as mothers, daughters, sisters, paternal aunts, and maternal aunts.
  • Ritual bathing after sexual impurity (الاغتسال من الجنابة - al-Ghusl min al-Janabah).
  • Circumcision (الختان - al-Khitan) for males and females, although it was marred by some incorrect practices.
  • Some practices of fitrah (الفطرة - natural disposition) such as trimming nails, shaving pubic hair, plucking armpit hair, letting the beard grow, and using the Siwak (السواك - tooth-stick).
  • Hajj (الحج) and 'Umrah (العمرة) to the Sacred House, although they had introduced many innovations (البدع - al-Bida') and polytheistic practices (الشركيات - ash-Shirkiyyat) into them.

Alongside these rituals, there were individuals in the Arabian Peninsula known as "الحنفاء" (al-Hunafa'), who remained on something of the religion of Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him). They worshipped Allah alone, rejected idol worship, and did not follow Judaism or Christianity in their distorted forms. Among them were Zayd ibn 'Amr ibn Nufayl (زيد بن عمرو بن نفيل), Umayyah ibn Abi as-Salt (أمية بن أبي الصلت) (at one stage of his life), Waraqah ibn Nawfal (ورقة بن نوفل) (who had extensive knowledge of the books of the People of the Book before converting to Christianity), and Quss ibn Sa'idah al-Iyadi (قس بن ساعدة الإيادي), and others who were seeking the path of truth. All these were remnants of the religion of Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him). Islam came to purify them of impurities, restore them to their pure origin, and make them part of its complete and eternal law.

Recap: Social & Moral Landscape

Vices: Widespread prohibitions (alcohol, gambling), financial corruption, various forms of adultery (Akhdan, Istibda', Rayat, Raht, Badal), female infanticide, killing children due to poverty, contempt for women (no inheritance, uncontrolled marriage/divorce, Dhihar, Ila', inheriting wives), Tabarruj, tribalism, constant warfare & raids.

Virtues: Truthfulness, fulfilling promises, generosity, honoring guests, protecting neighbors, resilience, courage, aiding the oppressed (Hilf al-Fudul), veneration for Kaaba, remnants of Abrahamic rituals (marriage prohibitions, Ghusl, Khitan, Fitrah practices, Hajj/Umrah), presence of Hunafa'.

Third: Other Religions Prevalent Before Islam and Their Influence (A Comparative and Analytical View)

Paganism was not the only belief system adhered to by the people of the Arabian Peninsula and its neighboring regions. There were other religions, both heavenly and non-heavenly, with varying degrees of influence and prevalence. Studying these religions helps us understand the complex religious map that Islam faced at the beginning of its call, and how it dealt with the followers of these religions.

Christianity (النصرانية - an-Nasraniyyah) and its Limited Spread (Between Authenticity and Deviation)

Christianity, brought by Christ Jesus son of Mary (المسيح عيسى ابن مريم - al-Masih 'Isa ibn Maryam, peace be upon him), had spread in some areas surrounding the Arabian Peninsula, such as the Levant (بلاد الشام), Egypt (مصر), and Abyssinia (الحبشة). It also had a limited presence in some parts of the Arabian Peninsula itself, especially in Najran (نجران) in Yemen, where a large and strong Christian community existed. Christianity, in its origin, was a monotheistic heavenly religion, calling for the worship of Allah alone and affirming the message of Christ (peace be upon him) as one of Allah's prophets. However, over time, Christianity was subjected to much distortion and alteration, and pagan beliefs alien to the original teachings of Christ entered it.

  • The Doctrine of the Trinity (الابتعاد عن جوهر التوحيد - Departure from the Essence of Monotheism): One of the most prominent deviations that occurred in Christianity was the doctrine of the Trinity (التثليث - at-Tathleeth), which states that God is three persons (Father, Son, and Holy Spirit). This is a doctrine that contradicts the essence of monotheism (التوحيد - at-Tawhid) to which all heavenly messages called. The Quran strongly condemned this doctrine, saying:
    "They have certainly disbelieved who say, 'Allah is the third of three.' And there is no god except one God. And if they do not desist from what they are saying, there will surely afflict the disbelievers among them a painful punishment." (Quran, Al-Ma'idah المائدة: 73)
  • Deification of Christ and His Mother (الغلو في الأنبياء والصالحين - Exaggeration Regarding Prophets and Righteous People): Christians also exaggerated in their veneration of Christ (peace be upon him) and his mother, Mary the Virgin (مريم العذراء - Maryam al-'Adhra'), elevating them from the rank of humanity to the rank of divinity or semi-divinity. The Quran warned against this exaggeration, saying:
    "O People of the Scripture, do not commit excess in your religion or say about Allah except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, was but a messenger of Allah and His word which He directed to Mary and a soul [created at a command] from Him. So believe in Allah and His messengers. And do not say, 'Three'; desist – it is better for you. Indeed, Allah is but one God. Exalted is He above having a son." (Quran, An-Nisa' النساء: 171)
  • Worship of Saints, Images, and Statues (مظاهر وثنية - Pagan Manifestations): The worship of saints, and the use of their images and statues in churches, and seeking closeness to them through supplication and vows, also spread among Christians. This is one of the manifestations of paganism that infiltrated Christianity.

When the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) was sent, Christianity in Najran (نجران) and elsewhere had deviated far from its original truth and had become a mixture of heavenly teachings and pagan beliefs. The Christians of Najran came as a delegation to the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) in Medina (المدينة) and debated with him about Christ. Allah then revealed verses from Surah Al 'Imran (سورة آل عمران) concerning them and called them to Mubahalah (المباهلة - mutual cursing), but they declined.

Judaism (اليهودية - al-Yahudiyyah) and its Transformation into a Closed Religion (Between Expectation and Obstinacy)

Judaism, which Allah revealed to Moses (موسى - peace be upon him), was a great heavenly religion, carrying a law and guidance for the Children of Israel (بني إسرائيل - Bani Isra'il). However, the Jews, over the ages, were subjected to many trials and tribulations, and their religion was subjected to distortion and alteration.

  • Geographical Spread of the Jews: Judaism was not widespread in the heart of the Arabian Peninsula but was concentrated in areas like Yemen (اليمن) and the Levant (بلاد الشام). However, after the severe events that befell the Jews, especially the destruction of their Temple in Jerusalem twice (once by Nebuchadnezzar (نبوخذ نصر) the Babylonian in 586 BC, and again by the Romans in 70 AD), many of them migrated and dispersed to various parts of the world, in what is known as the "Jewish Diaspora" (الشتات اليهودي - ash-Shatat al-Yahudi). A large number of these Jewish immigrants settled in some fertile oases in the north of the Arabian Peninsula, such as Yathrib (يثرب - Medina المنورة), Khaybar (خيبر), Fadak (فدك), and Tayma' (تيماء). They worked in agriculture, trade, and some handicrafts.
  • Awaiting the Last Prophet (بشارة وتحريف - Good Tidings and Distortion): The Jews, as mentioned in their books, were awaiting the advent of a final prophet. Many of them hoped or expected him to be from among the Children of Israel to restore their glory and dominion. They used to seek victory (يستفتحون - yastaftihun) through the advent of this prophet against their polytheistic Arab enemies. The Quran referred to this expectation in His saying:
    "And when there came to them a Book from Allah confirming that which was with them – although before they used to pray for victory against those who disbelieved – but [then] when there came to them that which they recognized, they disbelieved in it; so the curse of Allah will be upon the disbelievers." (Quran, Al-Baqarah البقرة: 89)
    However, when Prophet Muhammad (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) was sent from among the Arabs, and not from the Children of Israel, most of them disbelieved in him out of envy, obstinacy, and arrogance, even though they recognized him as they recognized their own sons, due to the descriptions of him they found in their books.
  • Distortion of the Torah and Departure from the Essence of Religion: One of the most prominent deviations of the Jews was their distortion of their holy book, the Torah (التوراة), and their alteration of Allah's words. The Quran referred to this distortion in several places, saying:
    "Do you covet [the hope, O believers], that they would believe for you while a party of them used to hear the words of Allah and then distort the Torah after they had understood it while they were knowing?" (Quran, Al-Baqarah البقرة: 75)
    The Jews also departed from the essence of religion, focused on appearances and superficialities, became fanatical about their race and nationality, considered themselves God's chosen people, and believed other nations were inferior to them.
Zoroastrianism (المجوسية - al-Majusiyyah) (Fire Worship in Persia - بلاد فارس)

Zoroastrianism was the official religion of the Sasanian Persian Empire (الإمبراطورية الفارسية الساسانية), which controlled vast territories adjacent to the Arabian Peninsula. Zoroastrianism is based on the worship of fire, considering it a symbol of God or one of His manifestations. Zoroastrians also believe in the existence of two conflicting deities: the god of good (Ahura Mazda - أهورامزدا) and the god of evil (Ahriman - أهريمان). Zoroastrianism had a limited influence on some Arab tribes neighboring Persia but did not spread widely in the heart of the Arabian Peninsula.

Sabaeans (الصابئة - as-Sabi'ah) (Worship of Planets and Stars)

The Sabaeans were a religious group that worshipped planets and stars, believing they had an influence on people's lives and destinies. They had a presence in some regions of Iraq (العراق) and the Arabian Peninsula.

Dahriyyun (الدهريون - Materialists/Atheists) (Denial of the Creator and Resurrection)

There was a group of people, known as Dahriyyun (الدهريون) or Zindiqs (الزنادقة - heretics), who denied the existence of the Creator. They believed that the world was ancient and eternal, and that there was no resurrection or reckoning after death. The Quran referred to their saying:

"And they say, 'There is not but our worldly life; we die and live, and nothing destroys us except time.' And they have of that no knowledge; they are only assuming." (Quran, Al-Jathiyah الجاثية: 24)

Fourth: The Spread of Polytheism (الشرك - ash-Shirk) and the Alteration of the Religion of Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him) (Roots of the Doctrinal Crisis)

The Arabs, as we mentioned earlier, were originally on the religion of their father Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him), which is the religion of pure monotheism (التوحيد الخالص - at-Tawhid al-Khalis), based on worshipping Allah alone without any partner, and rejecting all forms of polytheism and paganism. However, with the passage of time and the distance from that era, polytheism gradually crept into their beliefs until it became the dominant religion in most parts of the Arabian Peninsula at the dawn of Islam.

'Amr ibn Luhayy al-Khuza'i (عمرو بن لُحَيّ الخُزاعي) (The Pioneer of Paganism in the Arabian Peninsula)

Historical sources agree that the first person to introduce idol worship into the Arabian Peninsula and change the religion of Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him) was a man named 'Amr ibn Luhayy al-Khuza'i (عمرو بن لحي الخزاعي). 'Amr ibn Luhayy was an obeyed master among his people and enjoyed a prestigious position among the Arabs. He traveled on one of his journeys to the Levant (بلاد الشام), where he saw its people worshipping idols, seeking closeness to them, and believing that they interceded for them with Allah. He approved of this matter, and Satan made this heinous act seem attractive to him. He returned to Mecca (مكة) with some idols, set them up around the Kaaba (الكعبة), and called people to worship them and seek closeness to them. The first major idol he brought was "Hubal" (هُبَل), which became one of the most famous and greatest idols of Quraysh (قريش). Then, idols proliferated, and their worship spread among various Arab tribes, and every tribe had its own idol that it worshipped and sought closeness to. It is said that jinn (الجن) guided 'Amr ibn Luhayy (عمرو بن لحي) to the locations of the idols of the people of Noah (نوح - peace be upon him) (Wadd ود, Suwa' سواع, Yaghuth يغوث, Ya'uq يعوق, and Nasr نسر), which were buried in the Jeddah (جدة) area after the flood. He unearthed them and distributed them among the Arab tribes. Thus, the Arabian Peninsula, which was a beacon of monotheism, turned into a hotbed of polytheism and paganism.

The Spread and Multiplicity of Idols (مظاهر الشرك - Manifestations of Polytheism)

Idol worship was not limited to a few idols; rather, idols multiplied and diversified, and every tribe, and sometimes every household, had its own idol. These idols were made of stone, wood, or even dates. They believed that these idols brought them closer to Allah and interceded for them with Him. The Quran mentioned the names of some of these idols, saying:

"So have you considered al-Lat (اللَّاتَ) and al-'Uzza (الْعُزَّىٰ)? And Manat (وَمَنَاةَ), the other, the third?" (Quran, An-Najm النجم: 19-20)

Al-Lat (اللات) was a white inscribed rock in Ta'if (الطائف), worshipped by Thaqif (ثقيف). Al-'Uzza (العزى) was a tree in Nakhlah (نخلة), worshipped by Quraysh (قريش) and Kinanah (كنانة). Manat (مناة) was a rock at al-Mushallal (المشلل) near Qudayd (قديد), worshipped by the Aws (الأوس) and Khazraj (الخزرج). The number of idols around the Kaaba (الكعبة) at the time of the conquest of Mecca (فتح مكة) was three hundred and sixty, which indicates the extent to which polytheism and idol worship had spread in that era.

Altering the Rituals of Abraham's Religion (تحريف الحنيفية - Distortion of Hanifiyyah)

'Amr ibn Luhayy (عمرو بن لحي) did not stop at spreading idol worship but also changed some of the rituals that remained from the religion of Abraham (إبراهيم - peace be upon him). He innovated matters concerning livestock, forbade what Allah had permitted, and permitted what Allah had forbidden.

Al-Bahirah (البحيرة), As-Sa'ibah (السائبة), Al-Wasilah (الوصيلة), and Al-Hami (الحامي) (Innovations Concerning Livestock)

Among the most prominent of these innovations was what he innovated concerning livestock, such as:

  • Al-Bahirah (البحيرة): A she-camel whose ear was slit if she gave birth to a certain number of offspring (usually five, the last being male), then she was left alone, not ridden, her milk not drunk, and she was dedicated to idols.
  • As-Sa'ibah (السائبة): A she-camel let loose for a vow, for a sick person's recovery, or for other reasons. She was not to be utilized and was left to graze wherever she wished.
  • Al-Wasilah (الوصيلة): A ewe that gave birth to a female after a female (seven consecutive female births without a male in between). Her sister was joined to her (i.e., also dedicated), and she was not slaughtered; it was said, "She has joined her sister."
  • Al-Hami (الحامي): A male camel that had sired a certain number of females (usually ten). His back was protected; he was not ridden or burdened, and he was left for the idols.

The Quran condemned these pre-Islamic innovations and considered them fabrications against Allah, saying:

"Allah has not appointed [such innovations as] Bahirah or Sa'ibah or Wasilah or Hami. But those who disbelieve invent falsehood about Allah, and most of them do not reason." (Quran, Al-Ma'idah المائدة: 103)
An-Nasi' (النسيء) (Tampering with the Sacred Months)

Another of their innovations was An-Nasi' (النسيء), which was postponing the sanctity of the month of Muharram (محرم) to the month of Safar (صفر), or advancing it, according to their whims and interests. If they wanted to fight in Muharram, which is one of the sacred months, they would declare it permissible and make another month sacred in its place. The Quran condemned this practice and considered it an increase in disbelief, saying:

"Indeed, the postponing [of restriction within sacred months] is an increase in disbelief by which those who have disbelieved are led astray. They make it lawful one year and unlawful another year to correspond to the number made unlawful by Allah and [thereby] make lawful what Allah has made unlawful. Made pleasing to them is the evil of their deeds. And Allah does not guide the disbelieving people." (Quran, At-Tawbah التوبة: 37)

The Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) delivered a sermon during the Farewell Pilgrimage (حجة الوداع - Hajjat al-Wada') and announced that time had returned to its original state as Allah created it on the day He created the heavens and the earth, and he abolished the custom of An-Nasi' (النسيء).

Circumambulating the House Naked (انتهاك لقدسية الحرم - Violation of the Sanctity of the Haram)

One of the customs of the people of Mecca (مكة) (the الحُمْس - al-Hums) was that they would prevent those who came for Hajj (الحج) or 'Umrah (العمرة) from outside Mecca from circumambulating the House in the clothes they came in, under the pretext that these were clothes in which they had disobeyed Allah. So, they would oblige them either to borrow clothes from the people of Mecca, buy new clothes from them, or circumambulate the House naked. Men would circumambulate naked, and women would circumambulate naked except for a small piece of cloth covering their private parts. This shows the extent of ignorance and misguidance these people had reached, as they violated the sanctity of the Sacred House with these heinous practices. The Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH) forbade this custom and ordered that no one should circumambulate the House naked after the year of the Hajj led by Abu Bakr As-Siddiq (أبو بكر الصديق - May Allah be pleased with him) (the ninth year of Hijrah).

Al-Istiqsam bil-Azlam (الاستقسام بالأزلام) (Divination and Seeking Knowledge of the Unseen)

Another of their customs was Al-Istiqsam bil-Azlam (الاستقسام بالأزلام - divining by arrows). These were three featherless arrows; on one was written "Do," on the second "Do not do," and the third was blank. If one of them wanted to undertake an important matter, such as travel, marriage, or trade, he would go to the custodian (سادن - sadin) of the Kaaba (الكعبة), give him a gift, and then ask him to draw the arrows for him. The custodian would draw an arrow. If "Do" came out, he would proceed with the matter. If "Do not do" came out, he would refrain. If the blank arrow came out, he would repeat the draw. This is a type of soothsaying and seeking knowledge of the unseen from other than Allah. Islam forbade it and considered it the work of Satan. Allah Almighty said:

"O you who have believed, indeed, intoxicants, gambling, [sacrificing on] stone alters [to other than Allah], and divining arrows are but defilement from the work of Satan, so avoid it that you may be successful." (Quran, Al-Ma'idah المائدة: 90)

Islam prescribed instead the Istikharah prayer (صلاة الاستخارة), where a Muslim prays two rak'ahs and then supplicates to Allah Almighty to choose what is best for him.

Summary of the Religious and Doctrinal Situation

The religious and doctrinal situation in the Arabian Peninsula before Islam was characterized by polytheism, paganism, ignorance, and misguidance. Despite the presence of remnants of the tolerant Hanifiyyah (الحنيفية), they had been obscured by the innovations and deviations introduced by 'Amr ibn Luhayy (عمرو بن لحي) and others. Minds were captive to superstitions and illusions, and hearts were attached to other than Allah. There was an urgent need for a new heavenly message to return people to the innate disposition of monotheism (فطرة التوحيد - fitrat at-Tawhid) and to bring them out of the worship of created beings to the worship of the Lord of created beings.

Recap: Religious & Doctrinal State

  • Other Religions:
    • Christianity: Limited spread, doctrinal deviations (Trinity, deification of Jesus & Mary, saint/image worship).
    • Judaism: Concentrated in oases (Yathrib, Khaybar), awaited Prophet but rejected Muhammad (PBUH), Torah distortion, racial fanaticism.
    • Zoroastrianism: Persian Empire, fire worship, dualism, limited Arab influence.
    • Sabaeans: Star/planet worship.
    • Dahriyyun: Atheists/materialists, denied Creator & resurrection.
  • Dominant Polytheism in Arabia:
    • Introduced by 'Amr ibn Luhayy al-Khuza'i (brought Hubal).
    • Proliferation of idols (Al-Lat, Al-'Uzza, Manat, 360 around Kaaba).
    • Alteration of Abrahamic rituals (Bahirah, Sa'ibah, Wasilah, Hami).
    • An-Nasi' (tampering with sacred months).
    • Naked circumambulation of Kaaba.
    • Al-Istiqsam bil-Azlam (divining by arrows).
  • Overall: Deep-seated polytheism, paganism, ignorance, urgent need for monotheistic revival.

Preparing for the Transition to the Dawn of Prophethood

O brothers and sisters, after this detailed review of the conditions of the world, especially the Arabian Peninsula, before the mission of Prophet Muhammad (صلى الله عليه وسلم - PBUH), it becomes clear to us the extent of the darkness that enveloped the corners of the earth. Studying the Biography of the Prophet (PBUH): The Fifth Session

Harbingers of the True Dawn: Abd al-Muttalib, the Elephant, and the Glad Tidings of the Birth of the Best of Creation

Introduction: The Silence of the Universe Before the Break of Dawn

The greatest events in human history do not occur by chance, nor do they emerge from a vacuum. Rather, they are like the true dawn, which is preceded by a deep silence in the heart of the night, after which the first threads of light begin to sneak shyly across the horizon, announcing the imminent birth of a new day. Such was the birth of the Master of Creation, the Seal of the Prophets and Messengers, Muhammad ibn Abdullah (محمد بن عبد الله), peace and blessings be upon him. It was not merely the birth of a child in a barren valley; it was the culmination of a long and meticulously orchestrated series of divine preparations, a manifestation of His saying in the Hadith Qudsi:

"Indeed, My mercy precedes My wrath" (إِنَّ رَحْمَتِي سَبَقَتْ غَضَبِي).

It was a turning point destined to change the face of the earth forever.

In the decades preceding this blessed birth, the Arabian Peninsula, and Makkah (مكة المكرمة) in particular, witnessed momentous events that served as harbingers (Irhasat - إرهاصات) and glad tidings. These events centered around the formidable personality of the Prophet's grandfather, "Shaybat al-Hamd" (شيبة الحمد), known as Abd al-Muttalib (عبد المطلب), and reached their zenith in miraculous occurrences that shook the foundations of the ancient world. They proved that this Ancient House (the Kaaba) and this blessed land were unlike any other place on earth. This extensive article will delve into the depths of these precursors, analyzing their historical context, narrating their documented details, and extracting their profound significance, so that we may understand how Allah, the Exalted, prepared the cosmic stage to receive His greatest message.

Chapter One: The Arabian Peninsula at a Crossroads - The Historical and Religious Context

To grasp the magnitude of the events that would alter the course of history, we must first paint an accurate picture of the world of which the Arabian Peninsula and Makkah (مكة المكرمة) were a part. This land was not an isolated desert as one might assume; rather, it lay at the heart of an intense civilizational, geopolitical, and religious struggle between the superpowers that shaped the world of that era.

1. A World Rife with Conflict: The Persians, Romans, and Abyssinians

The sixth century CE was a stage for a bitter conflict between two great poles, each viewing itself as the center of the world and the sole possessor of absolute truth:

  • The Sassanian Persian Empire (to the east): An ancient empire centered in Persia (modern-day Iran), its dominion extended over Iraq and vast parts of Central Asia. Its official religion was Zoroastrianism (Majusiyyah - المجوسية), a faith based on the veneration of fire as a symbol of God, with a dualism between the god of good (Ahura Mazda) and the god of evil (Ahriman). Its system of governance was an absolute monarchy, based on the sanctification of the "Kisra" (كسرى) (the king's title), who was considered God's shadow on earth. Persia represented a center of organized paganism and materialistic philosophies.
  • The Byzantine Roman Empire (to the west): After the division of the Roman Empire, Byzantium, with its majestic capital Constantinople (القسطنطينية), inherited the wealthier and more powerful eastern part. It had adopted Christianity as its official religion, but on the Chalcedonian (Melkite) doctrine, which believed in two natures for Christ (divine and human). Byzantium saw itself as the protector of the Christian faith in the world and used religion as a tool to expand its political and military influence. The "Caesar" (القيصر) (the emperor) was the head of both church and state.
  • The Kingdom of Aksum (Abyssinia) (to the south): Between these two powers, a rising regional force emerged to the south across the Red Sea: the Kingdom of Aksum (مملكة أكسوم) in Abyssinia (modern-day Ethiopia). Abyssinia was also a powerful Christian kingdom, but it differed with Byzantium in creed, following the Jacobite (Monophysite) doctrine, which believed in a single nature for Christ. Due to its strategic location controlling the sea trade routes between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean, Abyssinia was a key player and a natural ally for Byzantium against Persian influence.

The Arabian Peninsula (الجزيرة العربية) was the buffer zone, the arena where these empires vied for influence.

  • In the North: The major Arab tribes were divided in their loyalties. The Ghassanids (الغساسنة) in the Levant were allies of the Romans, forming a barrier against the Persians. Meanwhile, the Lakhmids (المناذرة) in al-Hirah (Iraq) were allies of the Persians, forming a barrier against the Romans.
  • In the South (Yemen): Yemen (اليمن), "Arabia Felix," was the grand prize. Due to its wealth and strategic location, it was a field of bitter rivalry. It was invaded by the Abyssinians with Byzantine support (in the incident preceding the story of the People of the Ditch), and later, the Yemenis managed to expel them with Persian help, causing Yemen to fall under Persian influence. This constant fluctuation plunged the entire region into a state of political and social instability.

2. Makkah: The Beating Heart of the Peninsula in a Turbulent World

In the midst of this turbulent world, Makkah (مكة المكرمة) enjoyed an exceptional and unique status.

  • The Religious Center: It was not just a city but a sacred sanctuary (Haram - حرم آمن) and a spiritual center for all Arabs. Within it stood the Kaaba (الكعبة المشرفة), the Sacred House of Allah whose foundations were raised by Ibrahim (إبراهيم) and Isma'il (إسماعيل), peace be upon them, the symbol of the original monotheism. This Abrahamic legacy gave Makkah a special sanctity. Arabs would perform pilgrimage (Hajj) to it from all over the peninsula and honor its sacred months.
  • The Commercial Center: This religious status granted its people, the tribe of Quraysh (قبيلة قريش), a unique privilege. Their trade caravans traveled in complete safety on their winter journey (to Yemen) and summer journey (to the Levant), with no one daring to intercept them out of respect for the "People of Allah" and the neighbors of His House. This economic security, which Allah mentioned in His Holy Book, "For the accustomed security of the Quraysh" (لِإِيلافِ قُرَيْشٍ), made Makkah a prosperous commercial hub, a crossroads for routes and caravans.
  • The Darkness of Jahiliyyah: However, this lofty honor was mixed with the darkness of ignorance (Jahiliyyah) that had accumulated over the centuries.
    • Polytheism (Shirk - الشرك): Polytheism had crept into the hearts of the Arabs at the hands of Amr ibn Luhayy al-Khuza'i, and the worship of idols (الأصنام) spread around the Kaaba, until it was said their number reached three hundred and sixty, with each tribe having its own idol.
    • Deviant Morals: Detestable customs became common, such as the burying of newborn daughters (وأد البنات) for fear of disgrace, rampant usury (الربا) that consumed people's wealth, and odious tribalism (العصبية القبلية) that justified killing and looting for the honor of the tribe.
    • Remnants of Light: Nevertheless, the light had not completely vanished. Remnants of the faith of Ibrahim, Hanifiyyah (الحنيفية), which called for monotheism and the rejection of idols, remained in the souls of some wise people. The Arabs also retained noble human values such as generosity (الكرم) (even if it led them to poverty), courage (الشجاعة) in defending what is right, and fulfillment of covenants (الوفاء بالعهد) (even at the cost of their lives). These values were the fertile ground that would later embrace and nurture the seeds of Islam.

In this complex global and local context, where conflict was at its peak and darkness surrounded the remnants of light, the figure of Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim (عبد المطلب بن هاشم), the leader of Quraysh, emerged. It was his destiny to be the link between the ancient Abrahamic past that was almost lost and the bright Muhammadan future that would restore the Sacred House to its purity and the world to its guidance.

Recap: The Pre-Islamic World Context

  • Global Powers: Arabia was caught between the rival Persian and Byzantine Empires, with Abyssinia as a key regional player.
  • Makkah's Status: A unique religious and commercial hub, protected by the sanctity of the Kaaba but corrupted by polytheism.
  • A World in Need: Widespread political conflict and moral decay created the perfect conditions for a divine message of renewal.

Chapter Two: Abd al-Muttalib - Master of Quraysh and Guardian of the Ancient House

Abd al-Muttalib (عبد المطلب) was not merely a tribal chief but a formidable historical figure who combined wisdom, prestige, generosity, and an innate certainty. His original name was "Shaybat al-Hamd" (شيبة الحمد), so-named for a white streak (shaybah) in his hair at birth and because people praised (hamd) his deeds.

1. A Unique Personality in a Time of Darkness:

He was born in Yathrib (يثرب) (later Madinah), where his mother, Salma bint Amr of the Banu Najjar, resided. When he grew up, his uncle "al-Muttalib" brought him to Makkah. When the people of Makkah saw him walking behind his uncle, they thought he was his slave (abd) and said, "This is Abd al-Muttalib" (the slave of al-Muttalib), and the name stuck. Abd al-Muttalib inherited the leadership of Quraysh from his forefathers Hashim, Abd Manaf, and Qusayy, and he took on the duties of Siqayah (providing water to pilgrims) and Rifadah (feeding them).

Abd al-Muttalib was known for his unparalleled generosity, so much so that he was nicknamed "al-Fayyad" (الفياض) (The Bountiful). He would feed people on the plains and wild animals on the mountaintops. He was a man of great wisdom and sound judgment, to whom Quraysh turned in times of crisis. As for his religious beliefs, it is a delicate matter, but the opinion of the majority of scholars is that he outwardly followed the practices of his people during a time when there were no Messengers (Ahl al-Fatrah). This is evidenced by some of his actions that were part of Quraysh's customs, such as his famous vow at the idol "Hubal" (هُبَل) inside the Kaaba. However, despite this, Abd al-Muttalib was distinguished by a sound disposition and noble traits that made him rise above many of the customs of Jahiliyyah. It is narrated that he forbade wine for himself and prohibited his sons from injustice and oppression. Moreover, his timeless stance during the Year of the Elephant and his famous saying to Abraha, "The House has a Lord who will protect it," reveals a firm conviction in the existence of a great God, omnipotent over His creation, whom stone idols could not equal in power or status. Some narrations even mention that he would sometimes withdraw from his people for worship and contemplation (Tahannuth - التحنث) in the cave of Hira, a practice that distinguished seekers of truth in that era.

2. Reviving Zamzam - The Story of the Vision and the Confrontation:

One of the greatest honors with which Allah distinguished Abd al-Muttalib before Islam, and through which He elevated his status and that of his house, was the divine mission to re-dig the blessed well of Zamzam (زمزم). Its location had been lost and it had been buried for many centuries since the time of the Jurhum tribe.

The True Vision and the Divine Command

The story began, as great events do, with a true vision. Abd al-Muttalib was sleeping in the Hijr of the Kaaba, his most cherished spot, when a visitor came to him in a dream. He did not see the person but heard a clear, commanding voice: "Dig Taybah (طيبة)." Abd al-Muttalib awoke, wondering, "What is Taybah?" The following night, the visitor returned in his dream and said, "Dig Barrah (برّة)." His confusion grew, and he wondered, "What is Barrah?" On the third night, the command came more clearly: "Dig al-Madnunah (المضنونة)." He asked, "What is al-Madnunah?" Then, on the fourth night, the decisive and unambiguous command came:

"Dig Zamzam (زمزم), which never runs dry nor is condemned, watering the great pilgrimage."

The command did not stop there; it described its precise location with signs known to the people of Makkah: near the pecking of the white-footed crow (نقرة الغراب الأعصم), near the anthill (قرية النمل), and between the two famous idols where Quraysh used to offer their sacrifices, "Isaf" (إساف) and "Na'ila" (نائلة).

The Start of the Digging and the Confrontation with Quraysh

The next morning, Abd al-Muttalib, with his sound intuition, was certain that this was a command from God. At that time, he had only one son, al-Harith (الحارث). He took his pickaxe, accompanied his son, and went to the spot described in the vision. He began to dig, and in moments, the nobles of Quraysh gathered around him in protest.

They said to him: "What are you doing, Abd al-Muttalib? Are you digging in the place where we sacrifice to our gods? By Allah, we will not let you do this!"

Abd al-Muttalib was alone with only his son, while Quraysh stood with all its power and prestige. Here, his firm resolve became apparent. He turned to his son al-Harith and said with determination and steadfastness: "Defend me until I finish my digging, for by Allah, I will carry out what I have been commanded."

When they saw his insistence and knew he would not back down, and that he had no supporter but his son, they grew bolder and threatened him. At that moment, Abd al-Muttalib felt his weakness due to his lack of sons, and he made his famous vow: "If Allah grants me ten sons and they reach maturity with me to protect me, I will sacrifice one of them to the Kaaba."

The Journey to the Seeress and the Miracle in the Desert

The dispute intensified, and one party suggested they seek the judgment of a seeress of the Banu Sa'd on the outskirts of the Levant. All agreed. A delegation from every clan of Quraysh set out, along with Abd al-Muttalib and a group from his father's clan.

In the heart of a barren desert, under a scorching sun, the water that Abd al-Muttalib and his companions had ran out. They were overcome with such thirst that they were certain of their doom. They went to the Quraysh delegation and asked for water, but they refused, saying: "We are in a wilderness, and we fear for ourselves the same fate that has befallen you."

When Abd al-Muttalib despaired of people, his faith in God was manifest. He said to his companions: "What do you suggest?" They said: "Our opinion follows yours." He said: "I think each man should dig his own grave with his own hands while he still has strength. Whenever a man dies, his companions will bury him in his grave until only one man is left. The loss of one man is easier than the loss of an entire party." They did so, digging their graves and sitting down, waiting for death.

Then Abd al-Muttalib said to himself and his companions: "By Allah, to surrender ourselves to death like this, without trying to find a way, is sheer helplessness." He then stood up and said, "Let's move on!" He mounted his camel, and as soon as it stood up and lifted its hoof, a spring of sweet, fresh water gushed out from under the spot where its hoof had been.

When he and his companions saw this, they exclaimed "Allahu Akbar!" (God is Greatest), drank, and filled their waterskins. Then Abd al-Muttalib called out to the Quraysh delegation, who were watching from a distance: "Come to the water, for Allah has quenched our thirst." They came, astonished, and when they saw the clear sign, they said to him with one voice, their hearts filled with awe:

"By Allah, judgment has been passed in your favor against us, O Abd al-Muttalib. By Allah, we will never dispute with you over Zamzam again. The One who gave you this water in this wasteland is the One who gave you Zamzam. Return to your duty of providing water, rightly guided."
The Triumphant Return and the Emergence of Zamzam

Abd al-Muttalib returned to Makkah, having been granted a decisive victory by Allah without a fight. No one from Quraysh opposed him anymore. He completed the digging at the designated spot, and soon the blessed water appeared, and he exclaimed "Allahu Akbar!" in a loud voice.

And Zamzam was not the only treasure. During the excavation, he found the treasures that the Jurhum tribe had buried before their expulsion from Makkah: two swords, two coats of mail, and two gazelles of pure gold. Quraysh disputed with him over this treasure, so they resorted to casting lots. The two gazelles went to the Kaaba, the swords and armor went to Abd al-Muttalib, and Quraysh received nothing.

From that day on, the well of Zamzam once again provided water for the pilgrims, and the duty of providing water to the pilgrims (Siqayat al-Hajj) became an exclusive honor for Abd al-Muttalib and his descendants from Banu Hashim after him. Their status rose among the Arabs, and all of this was a divine preparation for the birth of the Master of Creation, Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him), from this noble house.

3. The Great Vow and the Ransom of the Second "Sacrifice":

In the throes of his loneliness and weakness before the clans of Quraysh, Abd al-Muttalib made a great vow: "If ten sons are born to me, and they reach maturity with me to protect me, I will surely sacrifice one of them to Allah at the Kaaba."

The days passed, and Allah blessed him with ten sons who grew into men, among them al-Harith (الحارث), Hamzah (حمزة), al-Abbas (العباس), Abu Talib (أبو طالب), Abu Lahab (أبو لهب), and Abdullah (عبد الله). When they were all grown, he gathered them and told them of his vow, and they agreed. He took them to Hubal, their chief idol, to cast lots among them. Each time, the arrow fell upon "Abdullah" (عبد الله), who was his youngest and most beloved son, a pure young man known for a light that shone from his face.

Abd al-Muttalib took the knife and led his son Abdullah by the hand to sacrifice him in fulfillment of his vow. Quraysh, Abdullah's maternal uncles, and his paternal uncles rose up in protest, saying, "By Allah, you will never slaughter him until we have exhausted every option. If you do this, men will continue to slaughter their sons, and what will become of the people then?"

After some debate, they advised him to go to a wise diviner. She asked them, "What is the blood money among you?" They said, "Ten camels." She said, "Then go back, place your kinsman and ten camels, and cast the lots. If it falls on your kinsman, add more camels until your Lord is satisfied. If it falls on the camels, then slaughter them in his stead."

They returned and did as she commanded. They cast the lots, and it fell on Abdullah. They added ten more camels, making it twenty, but the lot still fell on Abdullah. They continued to add ten by ten, and each time the arrow fell on Abdullah, while Abd al-Muttalib stood praying to God. This continued until the number of camels reached one hundred. When they cast the lots for the eleventh time, it finally fell on the camels. The people shouted with joy, but Abd al-Muttalib, to be certain, said, "No, by Allah, not until I cast it three more times." They cast the lots three times, and each time it fell on the one hundred camels. Only then did he order all of them to be slaughtered as a ransom for his son.

Thus, Abdullah was ransomed for one hundred camels and was called "al-Dhabih" (the Sacrificed One), just as his ancestor Isma'il was the "First Sacrificed One." This incident was another divine prelude; it purified this noble lineage from any bloodshed, elevated the status of Abdullah, and established the blood money for a human life at one hundred camels, a practice that Islam would later affirm.

Recap: The Role of Abd al-Muttalib

  • Leadership & Character: A noble, wise, and generous leader of Quraysh with deep, innate faith.
  • Revival of Zamzam: Guided by divine visions and a miraculous sign, he rediscovered the well of Zamzam, cementing his and his family's status.
  • The Great Vow: His devotion led to the ransoming of his son Abdullah (the Prophet's father) for 100 camels, a key event in purifying the prophetic lineage.

Chapter Three: The Year of the Elephant - The Day Allah Revealed His Great Sign

A few years after the ransoming of Abdullah, Makkah witnessed a momentous event that became a new calendar by which the Arabs dated their events: the "Year of the Elephant."

1. The Roots of Hatred and Ambition - The Building of al-Qullays

  • The Ruler: At that time, Yemen was under the rule of the Abyssinians (Kingdom of Aksum), and its governor was a man named Abraha al-Ashram al-Habashi (أبرهة الأشرم الحبشي). Abraha was a powerful and ambitious military commander who had come to power after eliminating his predecessor.
  • The Motive: Abraha observed with jealousy and envy how the hearts and wealth of the Arabs turned annually towards Makkah, to a simple stone house, the Kaaba (الكعبة المشرفة). He saw this as a religious and economic rally around Makkah and Quraysh, and he wanted to strip them of this status and transfer it to his capital, Sana'a.
  • The Project: To accomplish his plan, he decided to build a religious edifice to rival the Kaaba. He constructed a massive church in Sana'a, the likes of which had never been seen in that land, and named it "al-Qullays" (القُلَّيْس) (from qalansuwah, a tall hat, for its towering height). Historians described it as a marvel of luxury; it was built with fine marble, its doors were adorned with gold and silver, and it was encrusted with colorful mosaics and jewels.
  • The Declaration and Challenge: After completing it, Abraha wrote to the Negus (al-Najashi - النجاشي), the King of Abyssinia: "O King, I have built for you a church the like of which has never been built for any king before you, and I will not rest until I divert the pilgrimage of the Arabs to it." This was an explicit declaration of religious and economic war on Makkah.

2. The Spark of Anger and the Destructive March

  • The Arab Reaction: Abraha's action provoked the anger of the Arabs, who saw it as a blatant transgression against the house of their forefathers Ibrahim and Isma'il, which they had sanctified and revered for centuries.
  • The Incident of Humiliation: Narrations mention that a man from the tribe of Kinanah (كنانة) (or Banu Fuqaym) traveled to Sana'a, entered al-Qullays at night, and defecated in it as an expression of his contempt for and rejection of this project. When Abraha learned of this, he became enraged and said, "This was only done by a man from the people of that house to which the Arabs make pilgrimage, in order to anger me!"
  • The Oath and the Army: Abraha swore an oath to march to the Kaaba and demolish it stone by stone. He began to prepare a massive army, the likes of which the Arabian Peninsula had never seen, said to number sixty thousand soldiers.
  • The Terrifying Weapon: The most prominent feature of his army was a huge and formidable elephant named "Mahmud" (محمود). He was accompanied by several other elephants (said to be 8 or 12), but "Mahmud" was the leader and the greatest. The elephant was a terrifying psychological and physical weapon for the Arabs, who were not accustomed to fighting these creatures.
  • The Road to Makkah: On his way, Abraha faced some resistance from zealous Arab tribes. A man named Dhu Nafr (ذو نفر), a notable of Yemen, came out to fight him but was defeated and captured. Then, in the land of Khath'am, Nufayl ibn Habib al-Khath'ami (نُفيل بن حبيب الخثعمي) came out with his tribe and fought Abraha, but he also defeated them and captured Nufayl, keeping him alive to be his guide in the land of the Arabs.

3. The Historic Encounter between Abd al-Muttalib and Abraha

  • Arrival and Seizure: Abraha reached the area of "al-Mughammas" (المُغمّس) near Makkah and set up camp there. He sent a cavalry unit to raid the outskirts of Makkah and seize the people's property, which included two hundred camels belonging to Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim (عبد المطلب بن هاشم), the leader of Quraysh and the grandfather of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him).
  • Abraha's Message: Abraha sent his messenger, Hunatah al-Himyari (حُناطة الحِميري), to Makkah with a clear message: "I have not come to fight you. I have only come to demolish this House. If you do not stand in my way, I have no need for your blood."
  • Abd al-Muttalib's Stance: Abd al-Muttalib told the messenger: "By Allah, we do not wish to fight him, nor do we have the power to do so. This is the Sacred House of Allah and the house of His intimate friend, Ibrahim. If He protects it, it is His House and His Sanctuary. And if He allows you to reach it, then by Allah, we have no means to defend it."
  • The Meeting: Abd al-Muttalib went to meet Abraha. Abd al-Muttalib was a handsome, imposing, and venerable man. When Abraha saw him, he was impressed and held him in high regard. He descended from his royal throne, sat him beside him on a carpet, and said to his interpreter, "Ask him: what is your request?"
  • The Shocking Request: Instead of pleading for the Kaaba, Abd al-Muttalib said: "My request is that the king return to me the two hundred camels of mine that he has taken."
  • The Timeless Dialogue: Abraha was astonished and said with disdain: "You impressed me when I first saw you, but now I have lost all respect for you! You speak to me about two hundred camels I took from you, and you leave aside the matter of a house which is your religion and the religion of your forefathers, which I have come to demolish?"
    Here, Abd al-Muttalib uttered his words that have been immortalized by history, expressing complete trust in Allah:
    "I am the lord of the camels, and the House has a Lord who will protect it." (إني أنا ربُّ الإبل، وإن للبيتِ ربًّا سيحميه)
    Abraha was stunned by this certainty and said arrogantly, "He cannot prevent me."
    Abd al-Muttalib replied calmly, "You and that" (أنت وذاك) (meaning: that matter is between you and Him).

4. The Sign from the Heavens and the Soldiers of Allah

  • Supplication and Trust: Abraha returned the camels to Abd al-Muttalib. Abd al-Muttalib returned to Quraysh, told them what had happened, and ordered them to leave Makkah and take refuge in the mountain peaks and ravines to witness what Allah would do to their enemy. Then he and a group of Quraysh notables went to the Kaaba, clung to its curtains, and supplicated to Allah with a humble prayer. Among what Abd al-Muttalib said in his poetry was:
    "O Lord, I hope for none but You against them / O Lord, so protect Your sanctuary from them"
    "O Allah, a man protects his own property, so protect Yours / Let not their cross and their might ever overcome Your might"
  • The Elephant's Disobedience: The next morning, Abraha and his army prepared to enter Makkah. When they directed the elephant "Mahmud" towards the Kaaba, it knelt to the ground and refused to get up. They beat it with iron bars and pricked it with knives, but it refused to advance. When they directed it towards Yemen, the Levant, or any other direction, it would get up and run. But towards the Kaaba, it refused, by the command of Allah.
  • The Great Miracle: While they were in this state with the elephant, Allah sent against them His invincible soldiers. Flocks of birds appeared from the direction of the sea, not of the known birds of the Hijaz, coming in successive groups (Tayran Ababil - طيرًا أبابيل). Each bird carried three small stones, the size of a chickpea, made of Sijjeel (سجيل) (baked clay)—one in its beak and two in its feet.
  • The Inevitable Doom: The birds began to hover over the army and pelt them with these stones. They were marked stones; no stone fell on a man without piercing his helmet and body, destroying him. The massive army was transformed in moments into a scene of terror and death.
  • "And He made them like eaten straw" (فَجَعَلَهُمْ كَعَصْفٍ مَّأْكُولٍ): As the Quran describes, they became like the remnants of crops and dry leaves that animals have eaten and then excreted—a scene indicating total destruction and utter humiliation.

5. The Fate and the Lesson

  • The Death of Abraha: As for Abraha, Allah struck his body, and he fled with the remnants of his army, witnessing the punishment. His body began to fall apart piece by piece, finger by finger, until they brought him back to Sana'a while he was still alive to see the result of his arrogance. There, his chest split open from his heart, and he died a terrible death.
  • The Historical Impact: This incident was a landmark event, so much so that the Arabs made it a calendar to date by, saying, "So-and-so was born in the Year of the Elephant," or "Such-and-such happened ten years after the Year of the Elephant."
  • The Divine Wisdom: This sign had great wisdoms:
    1. Protection of the House: A divine declaration that this House has a sanctity that Allah Himself directly protects.
    2. Elevation of Quraysh's Status: The prestige of Quraysh was greatly magnified in the hearts of the Arabs, and they were nicknamed "Ahl-Allah" (أهل الله) (the People of Allah) because Allah Himself had defended them and their House.
    3. A Harbinger of Prophethood: This event was a prelude and a heavenly announcement (Irhas - إرهاص) of the imminent mission of the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him), who was born in the very same year, to draw the world's attention to this place from which the light of the final message would emerge.

Recap: The Year of the Elephant

  • The Aggressor: Abraha marched on Makkah with a great elephant to destroy the Kaaba.
  • The Guardian's Faith: Abd al-Muttalib showed complete trust in Allah, famously declaring, "The House has a Lord who will protect it."
  • The Divine Miracle: The elephant refused to advance, and flocks of birds destroyed Abraha's army with stones of baked clay.
  • The Significance: A direct divine protection of the Kaaba, elevating Quraysh's status and heralding the imminent arrival of the Prophet (PBUH).

Chapter Four: The Break of Dawn - Glad Tidings and Precursors of the Blessed Birth

In that atmosphere of awe that prevailed after the incident of the Elephant, when Allah had declared His power by protecting His House, divine destiny was preparing the world for the greatest event in its history. About fifty days after that incident, humanity had an appointment with the birth of the light that would dispel the darkness of ignorance and guide, by the permission of its Lord, to a straight path.

1. The Blessed Marriage and Early Orphanhood

Divine wisdom willed that this noble Prophet be an orphan, so that Allah Himself would fashion him and undertake his upbringing and care. Abdullah ibn Abd al-Muttalib (عبد الله بن عبد المطلب), the young man whom his father had ransomed for one hundred camels, married Lady Aminah bint Wahb (آمنة بنت وهب), who was at that time the most noble woman of her people, the Banu Zuhrah, in lineage, honor, and chastity.

But this worldly happiness was not to be completed, for Allah had a greater wisdom. Abdullah set out with a trade caravan of Quraysh heading to the Levant. On the way back, he passed through Yathrib (يثرب) (later Madinah), where his paternal grandmother's relatives from the Banu Najjar lived. He fell ill and stayed with them. He passed away there and was buried in Yathrib, before he could see his child, whom his wife Aminah was carrying in her womb. Thus, it was decreed for the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) to be born fatherless, under the sole care of Allah.

2. A Blessed Pregnancy and a True Vision

Lady Aminah was carrying the noblest of creation in her womb. It is narrated that she did not experience the heaviness and difficulty (wahm) that women usually experience during pregnancy; rather, it was a light and blessed pregnancy.

More established and important than that, she saw a true vision while she was pregnant with him, which is one of the early signs of prophethood. The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) himself spoke of this, as reported in an authentic hadith:

"My mother saw, when she was pregnant with me, as if a light came out of her that illuminated the palaces of Busra in the land of Sham (the Levant)."
(رأت أمي حين حملت بي كأنه خرج منها نورٌ أضاءت له قصور بُصرى من أرض الشام)

This vision was not just a dream but a great tiding and a symbolic sign with profound implications:

  • The Light: It is the light of guidance and Islam that would emerge from Makkah.
  • The Levant (al-Sham): The specific reference to the Levant carries great meaning; it was the destination of the first Islamic conquests, it is a blessed land, and it will be a stronghold of faith in the end times, as mentioned in authentic hadiths. The vision was thus a prophecy of the spread of his religion and the reach of his message to those distant lands.

3. The Moment of the Noble Birth

When Lady Aminah completed her term and her labor began, she gave birth to her blessed child, peace and blessings be upon him, on a Monday, in the month of Rabi' al-Awwal, in the Year of the Elephant, which is the most likely and famous opinion among scholars.

It has been mentioned in some narrations and reports by biographers that he was born circumcised and with his navel cord cut (مختونًا مسرورًا), and that when he was born, he fell onto his hands, leaning on the ground, with his head raised to the sky. Although these are matters that are not proven by an authentic chain of narration, they are mentioned in the context of the harbingers and miracles that preceded his mission.

4. The Grandfather's Joy and the Inspired Name

Lady Aminah sent a message to his grandfather Abd al-Muttalib (عبد المطلب) to give him the good news of his grandson's birth. The grandfather came, joyful and jubilant, for this child was the compensation for his deceased son, Abdullah. Abd al-Muttalib carried him and entered the Kaaba, where he circumambulated with him, prayed to Allah, and thanked Him for this great blessing.

Then came the time to choose a name. By divine inspiration, he chose a name that was not common among the Arabs: "Muhammad" (محمد). Quraysh was surprised by this name and asked him, "O Abu al-Harith, what prompted you to name him Muhammad, preferring it over the names of his forefathers and his people?"

He answered them with a statement indicating a sound disposition and profound insight: "I wanted him to be praised (yahmadahu) by Allah in the heavens, and to be praised (yahmadahu) by the creation on earth."

5. Cosmic Harbingers Pointing to a Great Event

Books of biography and history mention some wondrous cosmic events said to have occurred on the night of his birth. These events are known as "Irhasat" (الإرهاصات), which are signs that precede the appearance of a prophet. Although the narrations concerning them do not reach the level of authenticity, they are mentioned for context and for the symbolic meanings they carry:

  • The Shaking of Kisra's Palace: It is said that the Iwan of Kisra (the palace of the Persian king) trembled and its foundations shook, and fourteen of its balconies fell, as a sign that only fourteen kings remained of the Persian monarchy before it would perish.
  • The Extinguishing of the Persian Fire: This was the fire of the Magi (Zoroastrians) which they worshipped in Persia. It is said that it had not been extinguished for a thousand years, but it went out on that night, symbolizing the extinguishing of the fire of polytheism and paganism with the appearance of the light of monotheism.
  • The Drying Up of Lake Sawa: This was a lake that was considered sacred by them. It is said that its water receded and it dried up on that night.

These events, whether their narrations are authentic or not, their symbolic meaning is realized. His birth (peace and blessings be upon him) was a harbinger of the fall of the two greatest empires of that time (Persia and Rome), the end of the eras of polytheism and darkness, and the beginning of a new era in which the sun of guidance, justice, and mercy would shine upon the entire world.

Recap: The Blessed Birth

  • Lineage & Orphanhood: Born to Abdullah and Aminah, he was an orphan from birth, placing him under Allah's direct care.
  • The Mother's Vision: A light emanating from her illuminated the palaces of the Levant, prophesying the reach of his message.
  • The Name "Muhammad": An inspired and uncommon name meaning "The Praised One."
  • Cosmic Signs: Events like the shaking of Kisra's palace and the extinguishing of the Persian fire symbolized the end of the old world order.

Conclusion: From Harbingers to the Message

Thus, we see how the events that preceded the birth of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) were not random. From the renewal of the honor of the Banu Hashim by the re-digging of Zamzam, to the purification of his noble lineage through the ransom of his father, to the direct divine protection of his House and his tribe in the Year of the Elephant—every piece in this divine mosaic was placed precisely in its position to prepare the world to receive the greatest blessing and the most glorious message.

These events were necessary introductions, announcing that this coming Prophet was not a local prophet and that his message was not merely a tribal call. Rather, it was a universal message, preserved by Allah's protection and supported by His victory, which came to be a mercy to all the worlds. His birth (peace and blessings be upon him) was the true dawn that broke after a long wait, to dispel the darkness of ignorance and to illuminate for humanity its path to its Creator, in fulfillment of the divine promise:

"And We have not sent you, [O Muhammad], except as a mercy to the worlds."
(وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَاكَ إِلَّا رَحْمَةً لِّلْعَالَمِينَ)

Studying the Biography of the Prophet (PBUH): The Sixth Session

The Prophetic Biography as a Comprehensive Foundational Methodology: Before the Commission

Introduction: The Prophetic Biography as a Comprehensive Foundational Methodology

All praise is due to Allah, the Lord of the worlds, and may peace and blessings be upon the Seal of the Prophets and Messengers, our Prophet Muhammad, and upon his family and all his companions.

The noble Prophetic Biography (Seerah) represents a complete field of knowledge and a comprehensive way of life. It transcends the mere historical narration of events to form a practical embodiment of the principles and high values of Islam. Within its folds, the one seeking to purify their soul and rectify their heart finds their goal, just as the one aspiring to noble morals and high aspirations in worship finds their ideal role models. Similarly, it offers profound lessons in sacrifice and devotion, and in the methodologies of seeking knowledge and spreading the call to faith.

An in-depth study of the life of the Chosen One, peace and blessings be upon him, provides the Muslim with a frame of reference that shortens long distances in understanding and applying the religion. It presents the material of faith and education in its living, practical context, moving the recipient from the realm of abstract theory to the space of real-world application. From this perspective, the Prophetic Seerah can be considered a practical translation of the Holy Qur'an. This was perfectly summarized by the Mother of the Believers, Aisha, may Allah be pleased with her, when she was asked about the Prophet's character and replied, "His character was the Qur'an."

The ever-renewing nature of the challenges facing Muslim societies necessitates a rereading and re-examination of the Seerah from contemporary perspectives. The dilemmas faced by youth today—from issues of identity and a sense of value alienation to the challenges of intellectual doubts and material temptations—can find solutions and enlightenment through analyzing his stances, peace and blessings be upon him, and his methodology of patience, wisdom, and dealing with reality. In his biography, there is a cure for despair, guidance for the lost, and strength for the weak. This article analyzes a pivotal stage of his life, peace and blessings be upon him—the period before his divine commission—to clarify how the details of this phase constituted a precise divine preparation for the greatest mission entrusted to humanity.

The Birth of the Orphan: Deep Dimensions and Meanings

The Prophet Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, was born an orphan. His father, Abdullah, passed away while he was still in his mother's womb. This event was not merely a painful personal tragedy but a divine decree filled with profound wisdom and deep dimensions, for every detail in the lives of the prophets carries a specific message. These dimensions can be detailed as follows:

1. Preserving and Purifying the Natural Disposition (Fitrah)

The early death of his father formed a shield of divine protection for his sound nature, isolating him from the direct influence of the polytheistic environment that dominated Meccan society. Although Mecca held religious significance as a center of pilgrimage, it was steeped in the practices of ignorance (Jahiliyyah), from the worship of idols set up around the Kaaba to the prevalence of unjust economic practices like gambling and usury, and the dominance of tribalism that legitimized bloodshed. The absence of a polytheistic father from the Prophet's life, peace be upon him, prevented the transmission of these beliefs and practices during his formative years—the period when a child absorbs the values of his surroundings indiscriminately. His father had died upon the religion of his forefathers, which was the polytheism that Abd al-Muttalib followed. This is evidenced by the stance of his uncle, Abu Talib, who, on his deathbed, refused the Prophet's invitation to Islam, declaring he was "on the religion of Abd al-Muttalib." Had that religion been pure monotheism, this declaration would not have been a rejection of the call of Islam. Therefore, his orphanhood was a divine measure to keep his nature pure from any negative creedal tarnish. He was raised under Allah's protection; he never prostrated to an idol, never touched alcohol, and never participated in any of their frivolous or sinful gatherings. This meaning is reflected in Allah's words:

"Did He not find you an orphan and give [you] refuge?" (Qur'an, Ad-Duha: 6)
This refuge was a comprehensive creedal and spiritual shelter, preserving his heart and mind from the pollutions of ignorance, even before it was a physical shelter providing him with care.

2. A Purely Divine Upbringing

Divine wisdom dictated that the Prophet's upbringing and care be attributed to Allah alone, so that no human could claim credit or favor in his nurturing. This preempts any potential claim from someone who might say, "It was I who taught Muhammad and refined his character." He grew up as a unique "divine creation," prepared to carry the greatest trust without any earthly influence to which credit could be ascribed. Allah Almighty was the one who undertook his discipline and care. Every event he experienced, every personality he interacted with, and every trial he faced was part of a divine educational curriculum decreed with profound wisdom. His transfer from the care of his loving grandfather to his poor uncle, and from the life of Mecca to the desert of Banu Sa'd, were all stations in this divine methodology that polished his character. The purpose was that Allah's favor upon him would be complete and perfect, and that the Prophet, peace be upon him, would be in a state of complete servitude and gratitude to his Lord alone, not owing any essential favor to anyone else. This preparation also blocks any future suspicion that the wisdom and legislation he brought were the product of an acquired human culture; rather, it was pure revelation from Allah, a fact reinforced by his being unlettered, unable to read or write.

3. Comprehensive Human Consolation

In the orphanhood of the best of creation, peace and blessings be upon him, lies a deep consolation for every orphan and every pained soul across generations. No one should think that material deprivation or worldly tribulations are a sign of Allah's wrath or a person's lowliness in the eyes of their Creator. The most honorable of creation tasted the bitterness of being an orphan, serving as a message to everyone afflicted that a person's value with Allah is not measured by what they are given or denied in this world, but by their state with their Lord and the stations of patience and contentment they reach. It is a message of hope to everyone who has lost a support system in their life, that Allah is the All-Sufficient and the true support, and that orphanhood is not necessarily the end of the road but can be the starting point for a divine grant and a great gift. This consolation was later manifested in his practical conduct. When his cousin, Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, was martyred in the Battle of Mu'tah, the Prophet, peace be upon him, went to his house, embraced his children, wiped their heads, and his eyes filled with tears. He became a father to them after their father, translating the highest meanings of mercy and responsibility. His personal experience with orphanhood made him the closest in feeling to this group, so his care for them stemmed from a deep understanding of their psychological needs before their material ones.

4. Nurturing Mercy in the Prophetic Heart

He who experiences pain and deprivation is the most capable of sensing the pain and suffering of the deprived. Accordingly, the Prophet's orphanhood was a fundamental reason for him growing up with a tender heart, merciful to the weak and the needy. This mercy was not just a personal emotion; after his commission, it transformed into a complete legislative system that protects the orphan, preserves their rights, and commands kindness towards them. This is evident in Allah's words addressing him:

"So as for the orphan, do not oppress [him]. And as for the petitioner, do not repel [him]." (Qur'an, Ad-Duha: 9-10)
This is a direct instruction that links Allah's blessing upon him during his orphanhood with his duty towards those in a similar situation. A leader who has never known need might legislate laws devoid of feeling for people's suffering. But the Prophet's legislations, peace be upon him, were imbued with mercy because they stemmed from a heart that had tasted pain. He elevated the status of sponsoring an orphan to the highest ranks when he said,
"I and the one who sponsors an orphan will be like this in Paradise,"
and he gestured with his index and middle fingers, indicating the highest degree of closeness and companionship in Paradise. It is as if the sponsor of an orphan is emulating the greatest act Allah Almighty performed for His orphan Prophet: providing refuge and care.

As for his mother, she lived until he was six years old, giving him in these formative years a concentrated dose of affection and tenderness, an indispensable psychological element in building a sound and balanced personality. Her survival for this period was for a wisdom, just as her death afterward was for another wisdom. Glory be to Him who planned his affairs with the most minute detail.

Recap: The Wisdom of His Orphanhood

  • Preservation of Fitrah: Shielded him from polytheistic influences.
  • Divine Upbringing: Ensured his development was solely by Allah's design.
  • Human Consolation: A message of hope for every orphan and afflicted person.
  • Nurturing Mercy: His experience cultivated deep compassion for the weak.

The Nursing Period: In the Desert of Banu Sa'd

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was nursed by his mother, Aminah bint Wahb, for one week. Then, Thuwaybah, the freed slave of his uncle Abu Lahab, took over his nursing. Through this, he became a foster brother to his uncle Hamzah ibn Abd al-Muttalib and to Abu Salamah ibn Abd al-Asad al-Makhzumi.

It was a prevailing custom among the Arab nobles to send their children to the deserts to be raised there, aiming to achieve essential educational goals related to building a well-rounded character:

  • Eloquence of the Tongue and Purity of Language: The desert was the purest linguistic environment, far from the mixing of tongues and the influence of foreign dialects in commercial cities like Mecca. A child would grow up with a sound linguistic disposition, an eloquent tongue, and the ability to express himself in the purest form of his people's language. This masterful linguistic preparation was necessary for the one upon whom the Qur'an, the eternal rhetorical miracle, would be revealed, so that he himself would be the most eloquent speaker of the Arabic language.
  • Strength of Body and Clarity of Mind: The harsh desert life, with its pure air, clear sky, and simple food, strengthened and hardened bodies, raising young men to be tough, resilient, and capable of enduring hardships. Furthermore, the distance from the hustle and bustle of urban life provided mental clarity and strengthened the faculties of observation and contemplation of Allah's kingdom and His cosmic signs.
  • Authenticity of Morals and Values: In the desert environment, a child would absorb authentic Arab customs and noble values such as chivalry, generosity, providing aid, and fulfilling covenants, far from any external influences that might taint these characteristics. He learned courage from facing the dangers of the desert, generosity from its people who share the little they have, and dignity and self-respect.

In this context, women from the tribe of Banu Sa'd came to Mecca seeking infants to nurse, and among them was Halimah al-Sa'diyah.

Halimah al-Sa'diyah and the Accompanying Blessings: An Overflow from the Heavens in a Barren Land

It was a year of drought and severe famine that had consumed all that was green and dry, and destroyed crops and livestock. In these conditions, women from Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr, including Halimah bint Abi Dhu'ayb al-Sa'diyah, set out for Mecca seeking infants to nurse. It was the custom of Arab nobles to send their sons to the desert wastelands to be toughened by the harsh life, granting them strong bodies and eloquence of speech, away from the diseases of the cities.

Halimah was among the poorest of her people. She recounted her state, saying that she set out on a pale (white), emaciated she-donkey, which was so slow from weakness that it annoyed her companions. With her was an old she-camel (shārif) that, by Allah, did not produce a single drop of milk. She also had her infant son, who would not stop crying all night from severe hunger, finding nothing in his mother's breast to satisfy him, nor in their she-camel to nourish him. Despite this overwhelming material darkness, there was a light of hope in her heart. She would say, "But we were hoping for rain and relief from Allah." This represents a profound lesson in reliance on Allah (Tawakkul); the lesson is not in the weakness of material means, but in the strength of one's connection to the Causer of all means.

The Blessed Choice: When Light is Offered and Overlooked

The caravan arrived in Mecca. Every woman from Banu Sa'd was offered the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and each one refused him when told he was an orphan. They would say, "What can his mother and grandfather possibly do for us?" Their hope was in the generosity and gift of the infant's father. Thus, every one of Halimah's companions took an infant, and no infant was left in Mecca except that blessed orphan, Muhammad ibn Abdullah.

When it was time to depart, Halimah hated the thought of returning to her people empty-handed while all her friends had returned with a nursing infant. She said to her husband, al-Harith ibn Abd al-Uzza (also known as Abu Kabshah), "By Allah, I hate to go back among my friends without taking an infant. By Allah, I will go to that orphan and I will take him." Her husband, with a sound heart hoping for goodness, encouraged her, saying words that were the key to relief and blessings: "There is no harm in you doing so. Perhaps Allah will place a blessing for us in him." This statement came from a heart that saw with its insight what eyes could not see, and hoped for good where others had despaired.

Manifestations of Blessing: An Immediate, Tangible Outpouring

The moment Halimah took him, returned to her camp, and placed him in her lap, the divine blessings manifested in an immediate and tangible way, as if the heavens were waiting for this blessed choice to pour out their treasures:

  • An Instant Gush of Milk: As soon as she placed him in her lap, her breasts filled with as much milk as Allah willed. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, drank until he was satisfied. Then his foster brother (Halimah's son) drank until he was completely full, and they both slept peacefully. This was the first night her son had slept without crying in a long time. Halimah said, "My husband went to our old she-camel, and behold, her udder was full. He milked from it what he drank and I drank with him until we were both satisfied. We spent the best night."
  • The Strength of the She-Donkey on the Return Journey: On the way back, the state of the emaciated she-donkey that had been a burden on the caravan was transformed. It began to outpace all the strong animals, so much so that her companions were astonished and said to her, "O daughter of Abu Dhu'ayb, woe to you! Slow down for us! Is this not the same she-donkey you came on?" She would reply with pride and confidence, "Yes, by Allah, it is indeed her." They said, "By Allah, there is something great about her." They realized that something extraordinary had happened.
  • Blessing in the Lands and Flocks of Banu Sa'd: Halimah arrived in the lands of her people, which in that year was one of the most barren of Allah's lands. But after the arrival of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, everything changed. Halimah's flock would go to the pasture in the morning with the people's flocks, hungry and frail, but in the evening, they would return with full bellies and udders overflowing with milk. Her family would milk them and drink, while the flocks of the rest of the tribe returned in the same hungry state, not producing a drop of milk. This continued until it caught everyone's attention. They began instructing their shepherds, "Woe to you! Graze where the shepherd of the daughter of Abu Dhu'ayb grazes." They would follow her flock to the same pasture, but their flocks would return hungry as they had left, while hers returned full of milk. They realized that the blessing was not in the place, but in the one who had come to that place. His blessing, peace and blessings be upon him, extended beyond him to everything around him.

Growth Unlike Other Boys

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, remained with Banu Sa'd, growing in a way unlike other boys. He would grow in a day what others grew in a month, and in a month what others grew in a year. By the time he was two, he was a strong, well-built boy (jafr), eloquent in speech and clear in logic. When he completed two years, Halimah brought him to his mother, though she was the most eager of people for him to stay with her, because of the unending blessing she saw. She pleaded with his mother, Aminah, saying, "Let my son stay with me until he grows stronger, for I fear the epidemic of Mecca for him." She persisted until Aminah agreed, and Halimah returned with him, the happiest of people. His stay with them was extended, and the blessings continued, until the famous incident of the opening of the chest occurred when he was four or five years old. At that point, Halimah became fearful for him and returned him to his mother.

The story of his stay with Banu Sa'd was tangible, material proof that this child was not like any other child, and that his presence in any place was a cause for Allah's mercy and blessings to descend upon the people of that place. He is the same one who would later say as a Prophet, "I am the most eloquent of the Arabs, for I am from Quraysh, and I was nursed among the Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr," taking pride in that upbringing that polished his tongue and his body, and which was the first station in his preparation to carry the great message.

The Incident of the Opening of the Chest: A Heavenly Purification and Divine Preparation

The upbringing of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, in the desert of Banu Sa'd was not merely a period for gaining strength and eloquence; it was the stage for extraordinary events that prepared him for the greatest mission humanity has ever known. The most prominent of these events is the incident of the opening of the chest (Shaqq al-Sadr), which is considered the first practical and physical sign of his prophethood, and an operation of purification and preparation for his noble heart.

The most authentic and established narration, found in Sahih Muslim from Anas ibn Malik, may Allah be pleased with him, states that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was four or five years old. While he was playing with other boys behind the houses of Banu Sa'd, herding young sheep (bahm), the angel Jibril (Gabriel), peace be upon him, and another angel came to him in the form of two men wearing intensely white garments. The narrations mention that the other boys saw these two men approach Muhammad directly. They took him gently and laid him on the ground.

Terror struck the hearts of the children. They fled in panic to Halimah al-Sa'diyah, screaming at the top of their lungs, "Muhammad has been killed! Muhammad has been killed!" Halimah and her husband, al-Harith, rushed out in terror towards the place the boys pointed to, only to find him sitting alone, calm, but with the color of his face changed (muntaqa' al-lawn) from the ordeal.

As for what happened in their absence, Anas ibn Malik narrated from the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, that Jibril came to him, opened his chest, took out his heart, and then split it open. He removed from it a black clot (a piece of congealed blood) and said, "This is the portion of Satan from you." This was not a cleansing from sin, as prophets are infallible. Rather, it was a preemptive purification from the very origin of inclination and desire through which Satan enters the hearts of humans, so that his human nature would be in the highest degree of purity and perfection.

Then, Jibril washed his heart in a golden basin with Zamzam water—a blessed water in a blessed vessel—then sealed it (joined its parts) and returned it to its place. A physical mark of this incident remained as a testament to its reality. Anas ibn Malik, who served the Prophet for ten years, said, "I used to see the mark of that stitching on his chest."

This incident was not merely a miracle; it was a necessity to prepare his noble heart to be a pure vessel for the great divine revelation, so that Satan would have no way or entry point to him. It is said that the Seal of Prophethood—a gland the size of a pigeon's egg between his shoulder blades—appeared prominently after this incident as a physical sign of his prophethood, a sign that the People of the Book knew of from their scriptures and were awaiting in the Final Prophet.

After this extraordinary event, fear gripped the hearts of Halimah and her husband. It was not a fear of him being killed, but a fear that some harm from the jinn or spirits had befallen him. She said to her husband, "I fear that this boy has been afflicted, so let us return him to his family before it becomes apparent." She returned him to his mother, Aminah, in Mecca. When she told Aminah what had happened, Aminah showed no fear or panic. Instead, she received the news with remarkable steadfastness and certainty, saying reassuringly, "No, by Allah, Satan has no way to him. By Allah, this son of mine is destined for greatness." She then reminded Halimah of the vision she had seen when she was pregnant with him and when she gave birth, in which a light emerged from her that illuminated the palaces of Busra in the land of Syria. Thus, his mother was the first to believe in his great destiny and to be certain of his unique future.

Succession of Events: From One Guardianship to Another, From One Orphanhood to Another

Death of the Loving Mother (Age 6):

Two years after his return to his mother, Aminah wanted to visit her paternal uncles (the Prophet's grand-uncles) from the tribe of Banu al-Najjar in Yathrib (later Madinah), and to visit the grave of her husband, Abdullah, there. She took her six-year-old son and her servant, Barakah (Umm Ayman), with her. In Yathrib, he spent a month, saw the house where his father had died, and played with the boys of his grand-uncles. On the way back to Mecca, at a place called "al-Abwa'," his mother Aminah fell gravely ill and passed away.

There, in the desolation of the desert, the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, tasted the bitterness of orphanhood once more, having lost the father he never saw, and now the mother who was his entire world. He became an orphan of both parents. With this, the wisdom of the trial was completed, and his sense of detachment from all earthly support and reliance on heavenly support alone deepened. It is authentically reported in Sahih Muslim that he later visited her grave during the year of the Conquest of Mecca or the `Umrah of al-Qada', where he wept and made those around him weep. He said, "I asked my Lord for permission to seek forgiveness for her, but it was not granted to me. I asked His permission to visit her grave, and it was granted to me. So visit the graves, for they are a reminder of death." It is a scene that combines the majesty of prophethood with the tenderness of human mercy.

Under the Care of the Affectionate Grandfather: `Abd al-Muttalib (Ages 6–8):

After the six-year-old child buried the pure body of his mother in the soil of al-Abwa' and returned to Mecca with his caregiver, Umm Ayman, his small heart carried a deep wound and great pain; he was now an orphan of both parents. But the care of Allah that had surrounded him since his birth did not cease. It manifested in a new form, as he moved into the custody of his grandfather, `Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim, the leader of Quraysh and the revered elder of Mecca.

This guardianship was not merely a social duty; it was an outpouring of exceptional love and unparalleled tenderness. It was as if Allah intended to compensate this orphan with a love that filled the void of both father and mother, and to grant him, in the care of his grandfather, an honor and status that would protect him from the humiliation and hardship of being an orphan.

Abd al-Muttalib loved all his sons, but his love for Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, was something entirely different. The reliable books of Seerah, like Ibn Hisham's *Seerah* and Ibn Sa'd's *Al-Tabaqat Al-Kubra*, report that Abd al-Muttalib felt a tenderness for him that he had never felt for any of his own children. He would not eat a meal without saying, "Bring me my son," and he would be brought to eat with him. He would seat him by his side, pat his back, and delight in everything he did.

The most famous story illustrating this special status is that of the "cushion of leadership." Abd al-Muttalib had a special cushion placed for him in the shade of the Kaaba, where he would sit alone to manage the affairs of Quraysh. Out of respect for Abd al-Muttalib's great prestige, none of his sons would sit on that cushion; they sat around it in deference. However, the child Muhammad would come with innate confidence and composure and sit directly on that cushion. Each time, his uncles would rush to move him, but the affectionate grandfather would intervene, saying with a voice full of tenderness and certainty: "Leave my son be. By Allah, he is destined for a great matter." He would then seat him beside him on the cushion and caress his back. This recurring scene was a public declaration before the chiefs of Quraysh that this orphan was unlike any other.

When the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, reached the age of eight, his grandfather Abd al-Muttalib passed away. The child mourned him deeply and was seen walking behind his grandfather's funeral procession, weeping. But even in his final moments, Abd al-Muttalib planned for his grandson's future, entrusting him to his son, Abu Talib, the full brother of the Prophet's father, thus beginning a new phase of divine care.

To the House of the Protective Uncle: Abu Talib (Age 8 onwards):

With the passing of `Abd al-Muttalib, the eight-year-old boy moved from the house of the wealthy and prestigious leader of Mecca to the house of his uncle, Abu Talib—a noble and honorable man, but one of limited means and many children. This transition was, in itself, a new school in his divine preparation, teaching him to combine nobility of lineage with humility in living.

Despite his financial constraints, Abu Talib opened his heart and home to his nephew. His care was not mere shelter; it was an outpouring of sincere fatherly love that surpassed even the love for his own sons. It is narrated that Abu Talib would not sleep unless Muhammad was by his side and would not go out without taking him along.

As it had in the home of Halimah, blessing descended upon the house of Abu Talib, a tangible blessing witnessed by the entire household. His many children would often not have enough food to be satisfied. But Abu Talib noticed that when Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, ate with them, a small amount of food would be sufficient for all of them, with some left over. So, whenever a meal was served, he would say, "Stay where you are until my son arrives." The Prophet would come, begin his meal by mentioning Allah's name, and they would all eat until they were full, with food still remaining.

This blessed guardianship continued for over forty years, encompassing his youth, marriage, and, most importantly, the first ten years of his prophetic mission. After the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, proclaimed his call, Abu Talib transformed from a loving uncle into an impenetrable fortress and a shield protecting his nephew from the tyranny of Quraysh. Although he remained on the religion of his people and did not embrace Islam, he fulfilled the duty of protection and support like no one else. When the delegations of Quraysh came demanding he hand over his nephew to be killed, he famously responded with lines of poetry:
By Allah, they will never reach you with all their numbers,
Until I am buried, laid to rest in the dust.
So proclaim your message; there is no blame upon you.
Be joyful, and let your eyes find comfort in that.
Abu Talib's protection, based on the honor of his covenant and kinship, was a physical means that Allah established to preserve His call in its infancy. For this cause, he endured the unjust three-year siege in the "Valley of Abu Talib," boycotted his people, and suffered from hunger, all in defense of his nephew and his message.

Recap: Divine Preparation in Childhood

  • Desert Upbringing: Gained eloquence, physical strength, and authentic morals with Halimah al-Sa'diyah, accompanied by immense blessings.
  • Opening of the Chest: A direct physical and spiritual purification of his heart by angels, preparing him for prophethood.
  • Successive Guardianships: After his mother's death, he received immense love from his grandfather `Abd al-Muttalib and then unwavering protection from his uncle Abu Talib, demonstrating Allah's continuous care.

The Onset of Youth: Earning a Livelihood and Education in the School of the Desert

As the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, entered his youth, his remarkable character began to shine, and signs of early maturity and a sense of responsibility became apparent.

The First Journey to Syria and the Story of Bahira the Monk (Age 12):

When the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was twelve years old, his uncle Abu Talib decided to set out on a trading journey to Greater Syria (al-Sham) with a Quraysh caravan. The young Muhammad was deeply attached to his uncle, and when he saw him preparing to leave, he clung to the reins of his uncle's camel, expressing a strong desire to accompany him. Moved with compassion, Abu Talib said, "By Allah, I will take him with me; he shall not part from me, nor I from him."

The caravan set off and, on its way, stopped in the region of Busra in Syria. There, in a monastery, lived a Christian monk named Bahira, who was learned in the Gospels and had vast knowledge of the scriptures. Bahira had seen Quraysh caravans pass by every year without paying them any mind. But this year, he saw something astonishing. From his monastery, he noticed a cloud shading a specific young boy among all the men in the caravan, moving when he moved and stopping when he stopped. When the caravan dismounted to rest under a nearby tree, the branches of the tree bent down to provide more shade for that same boy.

Bahira realized these were signs of something great. He prepared a meal for them and invited the entire caravan, saying, "O people of Quraysh, I have prepared food for you, and I would like all of you to attend—your young and old, your slaves and your free." The men accepted, but they left Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, with their belongings due to his young age.

When they gathered, Bahira looked at their faces but did not find the signs he knew. He insisted, "Let no one remain behind!" They replied, "No one is left except a boy, the youngest among us." Bahira insisted, "Do not do so! Call him to join you." A man from Quraysh then went and brought him to sit with them.

Bahira observed him intently. After the meal, he approached the blessed boy and began asking him about his life, his sleep, and his affairs. The Prophet's answers corresponded exactly with what the monk had in his scriptures about the awaited prophet. Then, Bahira looked at his back and saw the "Seal of Prophethood" between his shoulder blades, exactly where he knew it should be, which confirmed his certainty.

The monk then turned to Abu Talib and asked, "What is this boy to you?" Abu Talib replied, "He is my son." Bahira said, "He cannot be your son, for this boy's father should not be alive." Abu Talib then clarified, "He is my nephew." Bahira asked, "What happened to his father?" He replied, "He died while his mother was pregnant with him." The monk affirmed, "You have spoken the truth." He then issued a timeless warning: "Take your nephew back to his land and guard him from the Jews. For by Allah, if they were to see him and know what I know, they would surely seek to harm him. A great destiny awaits this nephew of yours." Abu Talib quickly returned with him to Mecca. (This story was narrated by al-Tirmidhi, but some later hadith scholars consider its chain of transmission to be weak, though it is widely cited in books of Seerah).

Herding Sheep: The Profession of Prophets and a School of Leadership

As the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, grew up in the house of his uncle Abu Talib, who had little wealth and a large family, he felt a sense of responsibility early on. Motivated by his self-respect and desire to earn from his own labor, he began working as a shepherd, tending the flocks of the people of Mecca for a few qararit (a small fraction of a dinar or dirham). This humble work was a profound divine educational station, for there was no prophet who did not herd sheep.

It is authentically reported in Sahih al-Bukhari that he, peace and blessings be upon him, said:

"Allah did not send a prophet except that he was a shepherd."
His companions asked, "And you?" He replied, "Yes, I used to herd them for the people of Mecca for a few qararit."

The wisdom in herding sheep is immense, as it serves as a comprehensive training program for one who will lead nations:

  • Teaching Patience and Forbearance: Sheep are weak animals in need of constant care. They move slowly and can easily stray. Tending to them requires immense patience and forbearance—qualities a prophet needs to call his people, endure their harm, and remain patient with their obstinacy.
  • Nurturing Mercy and Compassion: A shepherd deals with the young, weak, and sick among the flock, showing them kindness and protecting them from wolves. This daily care cultivates mercy and compassion in his heart, which is the most prominent characteristic of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him:
    "And We have not sent you, [O Muhammad], except as a mercy to the worlds." (Qur'an, Al-Anbiya: 107)
  • Training in Leadership and Governance: A shepherd is a leader of his flock. He gathers those that have scattered, brings back those that have strayed, guides them to the best pastures and water sources, and protects them from dangers. These are the fundamentals of leadership: managing resources, maintaining unity, and ensuring security.
  • Instilling Humility: Shepherding is far removed from the displays of arrogance and pride common among the leaders of Quraysh. It is a profession that teaches humility and service to others.
  • Providing an Opportunity for Solitude and Contemplation: The life of a shepherd in the vast desert provides long hours of solitude, away from the noise of life and the idle talk of people. This solitude allows for deep reflection on the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, paving the way for the soul to receive divine revelation.

Thus, his youth was filled with lessons and experiences that built his unique character. Between a journey that revealed his status in previous scriptures and manual labor that taught him patience, mercy, and leadership, the divine preparation was continuous in every step of his life.

Conclusion: Crafted Under God's Watchful Eye

Every event in the childhood and youth of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was not random but was part of a meticulous divine plan for his upbringing and preparation. His orphanhood, his upbringing in the desert, the opening of his chest, and his labor with his own hands were all stages that polished his magnificent character and made him the perfect model for humanity. He was truly a "divine creation," crafted under Allah's watchful eye, as Allah said to Prophet Musa (Moses), peace be upon him:

"...so that you may be brought up under My eye." (Qur'an, Taha: 39)
His entire life before the commission was a prelude and a preparation for him to carry the greatest message and to guide, by his Lord's permission, to a straight path.
